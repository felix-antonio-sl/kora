# The Oxford Handbook of DBT — Evidence Base

Covers Chapters 20-24 of Section V. Systematic review of RCT quality, effectiveness in routine settings, cost-effectiveness, mechanisms of change, and client perspective.

## RCT Quality and Clinical Efficacy 1991-2015

**Authors:** Erin M. Miga, Andrada D. Neacsiu, Anita Lungu, Heidi L. Heard, Linda A. Dimeff

31 distinct RCTs conducted on DBT and DBT-skills-only through 2015, yielding ~38 publications. Studies rated using the RCT-PQRS scale (Kocsis et al., 2010; alpha = .87).

### Quality Rating Scale (RCT-PQRS)

25-item validated measure across six domains: description of subjects, definition/delivery of treatment, outcome measures, data analysis, treatment assignment, overall quality. Omnibus rating 1-7 (1 = exceptionally poor; 7 = exceptionally good). Criteria for top ratings: rigorous active control, Bonferroni + ITT analyses, blind reliable raters + standardized instruments, 1-year follow-up, justified conclusions.

### DBT Adherence Measurement

66-item instrument, 12 subscales, global adherence scale. Each item behaviourally defined, linked to adherence manual (Linehan & Korslund, 2003). "If-then" algorithm accounts for context. Adherence threshold: >= 4.0 (scale 0-5.0).

Training rating categories: NA (insufficient info), 0 (no formal), 1 (minimal: some workshop), 2 (moderate: some 10-day), 3 (maximal: all members completed 10-day before study).

### Key Findings from Developer-Conducted Trials

Linehan group conducted four RCTs for BPD (N=44, 101, 28, 33). DBT superior to TAU and CTBE in reducing NSSI, medical risk, hospitalizations, treatment dropouts, substance dependence remission, comorbid disorders. No significant DBT vs comparator differences in suicidal ideation, depression, hopelessness, shame, guilt, anger suppression, anxiety. CTBE findings indicate improvements resulted from specific active DBT ingredients, not therapeutic attention/expertise alone.

### Independent Replication Summary

McMain et al. 2009 (N=180): largest BPD RCT. DBT = GPM across most measures (suicidal behaviour, crisis services). Both conditions showed significant decreases. Benefits maintained at 2-year follow-up (McMain et al. 2012).

Clarkin et al. 2007: DBT = TFP = ST in reducing depression/anxiety. Only TFP significantly reduced irritability/assaultiveness.

Verheul et al. 2003 (N=58): DBT > TAU in reducing NSSI (especially among those with higher baseline NSSI) and fewer dropouts. DBT = TAU in substance use.

### Quality Findings Across All Studies

Mean quality rating across all 31 RCTs: range 2-7. Highest-rated study (7/7): Linehan et al. 2006 (only study with rigorous active control + all quality criteria met). Only 13 of 31 studies conducted formal DBT adherence ratings. Training level varied substantially: several studies used self-taught therapists (rating 0-1).

### Routine Care Key Findings

Suicidal/NSSI outcomes: Four controlled studies compared DBT vs TAU. All showed decreases in both groups; only Pasieczny & Connor 2011 and Priebe et al. 2012 found significant between-group differences favouring DBT.

Depression: Effect sizes ranged from small to large. Largest within-treatment effects: Pasieczny & Connor (d=1.3), Axelrod (d=1.08), Stiglmayr (d=0.99), Prendergast (d=0.92).

Hospital days: Consistent reductions across studies. Largest: Blennerhassett (57.83 to 3.83 days), Stiglmayr (d=0.61), Comtois (d=0.55).

Dropout rates: Higher in routine settings (mean ~25-30%) than efficacy RCTs (~19%). Range 0% (Brassington, very small caseloads) to 56% (Feigenbaum). Intensively trained therapists associated with significantly greater reductions in suicide attempts and NSSI (Pasieczny & Connor 2011).

### Adaptations in Routine Settings

Most common adaptations: phone coaching limited to office hours only (impact on outcomes unknown); shortened duration (5-6 months instead of 12); skills taught individually instead of group. Andion et al. 2012: individual DBT without skills group (N=37) vs standard DBT (N=14) — no significant difference between modes. Blackford & Love 2011: skills group alone for 6 months (N=18) — significant depression reduction; positive attendance-outcome correlation.

DBT-ACES (Comtois et al. 2010): Post-standard-DBT programme targeting employment. Significant improvement in competitive employment odds, school matriculation, working >= 20 hrs/week, subjective QoL. Gains largely maintained.

### Training and Adherence in Routine Settings

Teams with majority 10-day intensive-trained members + expert consultation produce comparable self-harm and hospitalization results to efficacy studies, but with higher dropout and more variability on other outcomes. Only 4 of 15 effectiveness studies used any form of adherence coding. No studies exist examining the relationship between low adherence and outcomes in DBT.

## Cost-Effectiveness for BPD

**Authors:** Roy Krawitz, Erin M. Miga

### BPD Service Burden

BPD lifetime prevalence: 2.7% (Tomko et al., 2014) to 5.9% (Grant et al., 2008). BPD represents 20% of psychiatric inpatients, 10% of community patients. BPD individuals: 42-50% of highest mental health service utilizers. Lifetime suicide rate ~10% (50x general population). Post-mortem psychological autopsy: 33% of suicides met BPD criteria.

### Meta-Analytic Evidence Summary

| Source | Findings |
|--------|----------|
| Ost 2008 | Moderate effect size d=0.62 for suicidal/self-injurious behaviour; marginally more effective than TAU for retention |
| Kliem, Kroger & Kosfelder 2010 | Effect sizes 0.37-0.51 (small-medium) depending on variables; moderate for suicidal/self-injurious when pooling RCT + non-RCT |
| Binks et al. 2006 | DBT decreases suicidal ideation and self-harm |
| Panos et al. 2014 | DBT efficacious in stabilizing/controlling self-destructive behaviour |
| Stoffers et al. 2012 (Cochrane) | Best meta-analytic evidence across all BPD treatments; DBT vs TAU: anger d=-0.83, self-injurious behaviour d=-0.54, general mental health d=0.65; no retention differences |

When comparing DBT to BPD-specific evidence-based treatments (Clarkin 2007; McMain 2009), effect sizes reduced to small and non-significant. No dropout differences between DBT and alternative BPD-specific treatments.

### Professional Endorsements

- NICE 2009: Only specialist treatment specifically named for BPD ("for women with BPD, for whom reducing recurrent self-harm is a priority, consider comprehensive DBT")
- Australian Psychological Society 2010: Level I (highest) evidence for DBT; Schema-focused and TFP at Level II
- SAMHSA 2011: Only therapy (besides psycho-educational multi-family groups) listed as evidence-based for BPD in report to US Congress
- APA Division 12 (2012): DBT is only current treatment with Level I strong evidence; MBT "modest," TFP "controversial"
- Sneed et al. 2012: DBT is the only treatment meeting criteria for "well-established treatment" for BPD (efficacy demonstrated by 3 independent groups)

### Cost-Effectiveness Data

Brazier et al. 2006 systematic review: 3 of 4 DBT trials showed DBT more cost-effective (53-85% probability). One exception: Koons et al. 2001 (less suicidal population) — TAU more cost-effective. Linehan & Heard 1999a: DBT resulted in US$9,000 fewer health costs than TAU, rated "good-quality" on BMJ checklist.

Brettschneider et al. 2014 systematic review: economic evidence limited; preliminary support for DBT cost-effectiveness. 67% of 15 reviewed trials used inpatient care as cost metric.

## Mechanisms of Change in DBT

**Authors:** Tali Boritz, Richard J. Zeifman, Shelley F. McMain

### Mediators vs Mechanisms

Mediator: construct demonstrating statistical relationship between intervention and outcome; suggests why change occurs but not how. Mechanism: describes pathway between mediator and outcome; how change comes about. Kazdin (2007, 2009) and Nock (2007) criteria for demonstrating mechanisms: strong association, specificity, consistency across studies, experimental manipulation, temporal relation (mediator changes before outcome), gradient (dose-response), plausibility/coherence.

Current evidence base: mostly correlational designs or secondary analyses of RCTs. No studies have employed experimental designs with random assignment + manipulation of proposed causal agent.

### Four Putative Mechanisms

1. **Increased awareness and acceptance of emotion**
2. **Increased attentional control**
3. **Increased ability to modulate emotion**
4. **Increased use of adaptive coping skills**

All operate within context of strong therapeutic alliance. Client-rated alliance consistently predicts outcome (Bedics et al. 2015; Hirsh et al. 2012; Turner 2000). Dialectical pattern of therapist interaction balancing validation (acceptance) and change predicts NSSI reduction (Bedics et al. 2012b).

### Evidence: Awareness and Acceptance

Berking et al. 2009: 81 female BPD outpatients from RCT (DBT vs TBE). Experiential avoidance (AAQ) associated with depression pre, during, post treatment. Higher experiential avoidance predicted less subsequent depression change. Decreases in experiential avoidance associated with decreases in depression over time.

### Evidence: Attentional Control (Mindfulness)

Soler et al. 2012: N=59 BPD, non-randomized. DBT-Mindfulness + GPM vs GPM alone. More minutes/week of formal mindfulness practice associated with improvements in depression, general psychopathology, confusion, and non-reactivity to inner experience.

Feliu-Soler et al. 2014: N=35 BPD. Greater mindfulness practice time strongly associated with improved depressive symptoms and lower self-reported (but not biologically indexed) emotional reactivity. Association with general psychopathology non-significant.

Perroud et al. 2012: N=52 BPD. 4-week intensive DBT + 10-month standard DBT. Significant increase in mindfulness-accepting-without-judgment over time (most pronounced during standard DBT). After adjusting for baseline BPD scores, increased acceptance without judgment associated with decreased BPD symptoms — but non-significant after correction for multiple comparisons.

### Evidence: Emotion Modulation (Neuroimaging)

Goodman et al. 2014: N=11 BPD + healthy controls, non-randomized. Pre-post 12-month DBT fMRI. DBT group: significant DERS improvement; decreased amygdala activation during emotionally adverse stimuli, associated with improved emotion regulation.

Schmitt et al. 2016: N=32 BPD females (12-week inpatient DBT) vs 24 healthy + 16 BPD controls. Pre-post fMRI reappraisal task: decreased amygdala, insular, anterior cingulate activity; increased connectivity in medial/superior frontal gyrus and superior temporal gyrus. Reduced anterior insula associated with improved affective dysregulation. Increased dorsal ACC associated with improved BPD symptoms and depression scores.

Winter et al. 2016: N=31 BPD (12-week DBT) vs 15 BPD TAU + 22 healthy controls. Decreased activity in right inferior parietal lobe/supramarginal gyrus during distraction task, correlated with decreased BPD severity. Treatment responders showed decreased anterior cingulate activity.

Schnell & Herpertz 2007: N=6 BPD (12-week inpatient DBT) vs 6 healthy controls. Decreased activation in anterior cingulate, posterior cingulate, insula. Favourable DBT response associated with decreased left amygdala and bilateral hippocampus activity.

McMain et al. 2013: N=129 BPD (from DBT vs GPM trial). Significantly improved affect balance (positive/negative symmetry) over time. Improved affect balance associated with decreased symptom distress and interpersonal problems, even after controlling for working alliance.

### Evidence: Adaptive Coping Skills

Neacsiu, Rizvi & Linehan 2010: N=108 BPD women from 3 RCTs (52 DBT, 56 controls). Increased DBT skills use (DBT-WCCL) mediated decreases in suicide attempts, NSSI, depressive symptoms, and increases in anger control over time.

Neacsiu et al. 2014: N=44, RCT. DBT-ST > ASG in skills use. Increased skills use mediated the relationship between condition and decreases in emotion dysregulation and anxiety severity.

Barnicot et al. 2016: N=70 BPD, non-randomized, 12-month DBT. Increased coping skills (self-report) associated with decreased self-harm, independent of treatment credibility, self-efficacy, and therapeutic alliance.

Stepp et al. 2008: N=27 BPD features, 12-month DBT. Weekly skills use (diary cards) associated with reduced total borderline symptoms, affective instability, identity disturbance, negative relationships. Greater mindfulness use marginally associated with decreased NSSI.

### Limitations

Most studies are secondary analyses of RCTs, not designed specifically for mechanisms research. Timing/frequency of mediator/outcome measurement suboptimal. Variability in operationalization of constructs (e.g., "mindfulness," "coping skills" are broad). Primary reliance on self-report measures. No experimental manipulation studies to date.

## Client Perspective on DBT

**Author:** Louise Brinton Clarke

First-person account of a BPD client completing two rounds of DBT modules (1 year as client, 1 year as graduate facilitator). History of childhood neglect, abuse, abandonment; care system from young age to 18. NSSI and substance misuse as validation-seeking and emotion regulation strategies. BPD diagnosis at age 15 (premature per author), officially diagnosed at 18. 17-year period of crisis behaviours before entering DBT at age 31.

### Treatment Experience

Prior therapeutic community taught thought/feeling awareness but not sufficient skills for sustained recovery. DBT individual therapy: initially resistant to structure and limits; therapist's directness, limit-setting, and willingness to challenge were essential. Walking out of early session resolved through therapist's creative, personalized outreach (pressed flower letter). Relationship = ~50% of therapy, skills programme = ~50%.

### Skills Outcomes

DEAR MAN (interpersonal effectiveness) — most beneficial single skill. Changed communication patterns, enabled self-validation, reduced argumentativeness. Mindfulness initially resisted (preconceived judgments), became daily practice. Distress tolerance and emotion regulation skills work in concert. Radical acceptance most difficult concept for participants.

### Recovery Markers

- 2 years without self-harm at time of writing (January 2018)
- Completed psychology degree (2:1) + postgraduate counselling diploma
- Voluntarily ceased state benefits, full-time employment (university mentor + residential rehab unit)
- Married (2016), passed driving test
- First formal planned discharge from community mental health team
- First depressive episode without resorting to self-harm behaviours

### Clinical Implications from Client View

BPD label can be more damaging than helpful; services maintaining diagnosis when criteria no longer met are unhelpful. DBT requires confident, competent practitioners willing to challenge and set limits. Skills rationale needs repeated explanation. Programme consistency essential — practitioners should commit to at least two cycles. Comorbid depression/anxiety should not be disregarded because of BPD diagnosis. Graduate facilitator role has potential but requires clear purpose, structure, and team support. Post-DBT trauma therapy access recommended but often unavailable. DBT addresses present functioning and skills but does not resolve underlying trauma.
