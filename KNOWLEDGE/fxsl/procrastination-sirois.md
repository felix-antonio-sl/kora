# Procrastination — Sirois (2022)


## Definition and Classification

**Standard definition:** Procrastination is the unnecessary and voluntary delay in starting or completing important intended tasks despite recognition that this delay may have negative consequences.

**Extended definition (Sirois & Pychyl, 2013):** Procrastination is a form of self-regulation failure that prioritizes short-term mood repair over long-term pursuit of intended actions.

**Mandatory criteria (all must be present):**

- Delay is unnecessary (no external force)
- Delay is voluntary (chosen)
- Task was intended
- Negative consequences are recognized

**What procrastination is NOT:**

- Sagacious (wise) delay: deliberate postponement due to missing resources, information, or support needed to succeed
- Involuntary delay: illness, emergency, external obstacle
- Reprioritization: legitimately shifting focus to a more urgent task
- Delay without prior intention to complete the task

**Active procrastination (myth):**

- Chu & Choi (2005) proposed "active procrastination" (purposeful last-minute work) as beneficial
- Refuted by Chowdhury & Pychyl (2018): lacks defining features of true procrastination
- Refuted by Pinxten et al. (2019): 1,600 students, no validity for scale, no academic benefit found
- Rationalizing last-minute behavior is post hoc self-esteem protection, not a strategy

## Prevalence

| Population | Rate | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Adults: habitual procrastinators | 15–25% | Steel, 2007 |
| University students: procrastinated at some point | 80–95% | Steel, 2007 |
| University students: procrastinate regularly | ~50% | Steel, 2007 |

Chronic (trait) procrastination = enduring personal tendency, not fixed; modifiable through intervention.

## Core Mechanism: Mood Regulation Model

**Process chain:**

1. Aversive task triggers negative emotion (boredom, anxiety, frustration, self-doubt, uncertainty)
2. Avoidance produces temporary mood repair (hedonic shift)
3. Trade-off: present emotional comfort at cost of future consequences
4. Thought suppression about avoided task backfires: increases focus on the avoided task and worsens mood

**Two criteria for effective emotion regulation (both failed by procrastination):**

1. Strategy must be effective (helps achieve goals)
2. Effects must be lasting (not temporary)

**Mood malleability belief — key variable (Tice et al., 2001):**

- Participants made sad + told mood was "frozen" → better self-control, less avoidance
- Participants made sad + told eating helps mood → more overeating, more avoidance
- Belief that mood can be changed drives procrastination risk; if mood is perceived as fixed, avoidance loses its function

**Temporal myopia:**

- Stress and threat activate amygdala → narrows attention to present moment
- Future consequences become psychologically abstract and distant
- Cycle: difficult task → stress response → temporal myopia → procrastination

**Construal level effect (McCrea et al., 2008):**

- Abstract thinking instructions → delayed task return
- Concrete thinking instructions → earlier task return
- Present is experienced as concrete; future as abstract; greater abstraction = greater psychological distance from consequences

## Time Perception and Future Self

**Future self-continuity:**

- Neuroimaging: distant future self activates same brain regions as a stranger's traits, not one's own (Ersner-Hershfield et al., 2009)
- Procrastinators feel more psychologically distant from future self than non-procrastinators
- Lower future self-continuity → less health-promoting behavior (Sirois et al., 2014)

**Measurement — Future Self Continuity Scale (Ersner-Hershfield et al., 2009):**

- Overlapping circles rated 1 (very distant, like a stranger) to 7 (very close, like a friend)

**Superhero-to-Beast pattern:**

- Procrastinators overestimate short-term personal change and push tasks to a "future superhero self" expected to be more capable
- Future self becomes the "beast of burden" carrying accumulated costs of delay
- This time-starving of the future self generates additional stress

## Consequences: Academic and Workplace

**Academic:**

- 75% of university students engaged in academic misconduct under deadline pressure (Patrzek et al., 2015: 3,000+ students, 4 universities)
- Forms: fraudulent excuses, false medical certificates, cheating, plagiarism, data falsification
- Postgraduate impact: delayed thesis/dissertation completion, longer time to degree

**Workplace:**

- ~50% of employees waste 4+ hours/day procrastinating (survey of 2,000+ full-time employees)
- Employer cost: $8,000–$10,000 per employee per year
- Gym membership abandonment: 50% within 6 months → $49M/year wasted

**Workplace forms:**

- Soldiering: offline procrastination (coffee breaks, socializing, daydreaming) exceeding 1 hour/day
- Cyberslacking: personal internet use during work hours

**Cyberslacking data (Metin et al., 2016):**

- 4 in 5 employees admit to cyberslacking
- 97% of men, 85% of women consider it acceptable
- Network security cost: ~$130,000/year per organization
- Drivers: feeling mistreated, boredom, job dissatisfaction

**Career impact (Kim et al., 2016: 22,000+ employees):**

- Chronic procrastinators: lower annual income, less stable employment, shorter job tenure
- Higher rates of part-time employment and unemployment
- Jobs with low autonomy and fewer advancement opportunities

## Consequences: Health

**Mental health:**

- Procrastination linked to anxiety, stress, depression, guilt, shame, self-loathing (Flett et al., 1995)
- Rumination: automatic, repetitive self-critical thinking; reactivates and amplifies negative emotions
- Rumination predicts development of anxiety and depression (McLaughlin & Nolen-Hoeksema, 2011)
- Procrastinatory cognitions: category of repetitive self-critical thoughts specific to procrastination episodes
- Chronic procrastinators less likely to seek mental health help (Stead et al., 2010); procrastination is the primary barrier to therapy access
- Combination of high procrastination + high stress is most damaging to mental health

**Internet use mediator (Reinecke et al., 2018: 1,600 users):**

- High trait procrastination → more time spent online, greater difficulty controlling internet use
- Belief that internet misuse is harmful mediates procrastination → poor mental health outcome

**Social media guilt (Myrick, 2015: 6,800 subjects):**

- Cat videos relieve stress effectively during legitimate breaks; generate guilt when used as procrastination avoidance
- Procrastinators experience guilt even during breaks they have legitimately earned

**Physical health:**

- Stress elevates cortisol → compromises immune system
- 1 week of sleep deprivation impairs immune function and elevates cortisol
- Bedtime procrastination: ~50% admit to at least weekly occurrence (Kroese et al., 2016)
- Sleep deprivation increases cravings for high-calorie food; undermines health behavior adherence

**Cardiovascular risk (Sirois, 2015: ~1,000 adults):**

- Every 1-point increase on 5-point procrastination scale = 63% increased risk of poor cardiovascular health
- Linked to hypertension and heart disease

**Health chain:** Chronic procrastination → Stress + decreased health behaviors (treatment delay, poor sleep, reduced health-promoting behaviors) → Poor physical health outcomes

## Consequences: Social and Relational

**Workplace relationships (van Eerde & Sirois, 2021: ~100 employees):**

- Procrastinating coworker: rated equally likeable but significantly less dependable
- Colleagues unwilling to partner with procrastinators on important projects

**Leadership impact (Legood et al., 2018: supervisors and employees, UK and Australia):**

- Procrastinating managers rated as less effective by direct reports
- Erodes trust in leadership
- Employees under procrastinating leaders: high frustration, low engagement
- Leader procrastination linked to employee deviance and poor organizational citizenship behaviors

**Social perception:**

- Procrastination violates norms of self-regulation and follow-through
- Automatic attributions: lazy, undisciplined, impulsive, unreliable
- Procrastinators apply same negative judgments to themselves

**Guilt vs. shame:**

- Guilt: behavior-focused ("I did something bad") → can motivate corrective action
- Shame: identity-focused ("I am bad") → drives withdrawal and further avoidance; adds second layer of avoidance onto procrastination

**Reducing guilt/shame cycle (Sirois & Yang, 2021: 200 community adults + 600 nurses):**

- Chronic procrastinators perceive less social support even when objectively available
- Being upfront about procrastination + requesting help + demonstrating accountability reduces shame and earns respect from others

## Sources of Negative Emotions

**Two trigger categories:**

1. Task evaluation: aversiveness of the task itself
2. Self-evaluation: beliefs about personal adequacy to complete the task

**Task aversiveness dimensions (Blunt & Pychyl, 1998, 2000):**

- Boredom, frustration, guilt, resentment
- Uncertainty: unclear how to proceed
- Low controllability, low confidence in completing the task
- Low personal meaning or identity relevance
- Low absorption potential
- Lack of structure

**Task journey stages where aversiveness is highest (Blunt & Pychyl, 2000):**

- Inception: linked to low personal meaning and anticipated stress
- During action: linked to low control and uncertainty
- Abandonment: 32% of homeowners abandon projects after >12 months (Schmall, 2018)

**Low self-esteem:**

- Amplifies difficulties as personal failures
- Reduces resilience to setbacks
- Creates feedback loop: procrastination erodes self-esteem → self-esteem erosion increases procrastination

**Self-esteem and task difficulty (Brown & Dutton, 1995):**

- Easy tasks: no performance difference between low and high SE individuals
- Difficult tasks: low SE → shame, humiliation, dissatisfaction; high SE → difficulty acknowledged without self-worth damage

**Perfectionism — two empirically distinct forms:**

| Form | Standards | Self-worth | Procrastination link |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfectionistic strivings | High personal standards | Stable | Negatively associated (adaptive) |
| Perfectionistic concerns | Unrealistic, self-critical | Contingent on outcomes | Positively associated (maladaptive) |

**Meta-analysis: perfectionism and procrastination (Sirois et al., 2017: 43 studies, 10,000 participants):**

- Self-critical perfectionists: significantly more prone to procrastination
- Perfectionistic strivers: significantly less prone to procrastination

**Self-criticism function and cost:**

- Function: temporarily restores perceived control over uncertainty
- Costs: lowers self-esteem, amplifies negative emotions, linked to depression and anxiety

**Irrational belief patterns (CBT framework, van Eerde & Klingsieck, 2018):**

- Often formed in childhood through interpretation of caregivers' responses
- Operate automatically and outside conscious awareness
- CBT effective for identifying, challenging, and replacing these beliefs

## Strategy: Self-Compassion

**Definition (Neff, 2011):** Responding to one's own difficulties with the same kindness extended to a struggling friend.

**Three components:**

1. Self-kindness vs. self-judgment
2. Common humanity vs. isolation (recognizing difficulty as shared human experience)
3. Mindfulness vs. over-identification with negative thoughts

**Self-compassion in procrastination:**

- Quiets the harsh inner critic
- Breaks the self-criticism → avoidance → procrastination cycle
- Increases motivation for self-improvement (Breines & Chen, 2012)
- Accepts negative feelings rather than suppressing them (suppression amplifies avoidance)
- Can be cultivated as an enduring mindset, not only applied in-the-moment

**Self-criticism paradox:** Excessive self-criticism to force improvement actually worsens procrastination by triggering demotivation and avoidance.

---

## Strategy: Self-Forgiveness

Self-forgiveness is the voluntary process of releasing negative emotions (guilt, shame, resentment) directed at oneself for harm caused by one's own procrastination. Distinct from self-compassion: self-compassion applies during the struggle or urge; self-forgiveness applies after procrastination has already occurred.

**Three-step journey to self-forgiveness (Wohl et al., 2008):**

1. Accept responsibility: acknowledge that you procrastinated voluntarily, without blaming external forces
2. Acknowledge negative feelings: allow yourself to experience guilt, disappointment, frustration about procrastinating — these feelings are natural and healthy
3. Shift from retribution to restitution: stop punishing yourself; focus on what you can do to make amends and reduce the chances of procrastinating again

**Empirical evidence (Wohl et al., 2010, Carleton University):**

- Students who forgave themselves for procrastinating studying for first midterm exam were less likely to procrastinate studying for second midterm
- Mechanism: self-forgiveness → less negative mood → reduced procrastination
- Students who did not forgive themselves felt worse about performance and continued to procrastinate

**Self-Compassion vs. Self-Forgiveness — when to use each:**

| Situation | Recommended approach | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Struggling with task, on brink of procrastinating, haven't procrastinated yet | Self-compassion | No transgression committed; manage brewing negative emotions to prevent procrastination |
| Already procrastinated; consequences are concrete or harmed others (e.g., missed tax deadline affecting partner) | Self-forgiveness | Transgression committed with significant impact; need to come to terms with consequences, release guilt, focus on amends |
| Already procrastinated; aftermath less concrete, personal distress | Either; self-compassion preferred | Soothe suffering, short-circuit rumination, regain balanced perspective and motivation |
