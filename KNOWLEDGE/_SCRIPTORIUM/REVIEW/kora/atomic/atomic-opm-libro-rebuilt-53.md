## opm libro · Chapter 18 Exhibition-Characterization / 18.1.1 Primary and Secondary Qualities

- **P2731** · `fact` · Many philosophers who discussed epistemology and metaphysics, including Galileo, Descartes, and Locke, have made the conceptual distinction between primary and secondary qualities (or properties, or attributes). · [src:S01:L7170-L7199](../../../INBOX/opm-libro.txt#L7170-L7199)
- **P2732** · `fact` · Primary qualities are “independent” properties of objects, such as shape or mass, which convey facts about the thing and do not rely on subjective judgments. · [src:S01:L7170-L7199](../../../INBOX/opm-libro.txt#L7170-L7199)
- **P2733** · `fact` · Secondary qualities are properties such as color, taste, smell, and sound, which depend on and produce sensations in observers and do not provide objective facts about things. · [src:S01:L7170-L7199](../../../INBOX/opm-libro.txt#L7170-L7199)
- **P2734** · `constraint` · As Galileo (1623) wrote in The Assayer, “… tastes, odors, colors, and so on are no more than mere names so far as the object in which we locate them are concerned, and that they reside in consciousness.” Further, Descartes (1647) wrote about secondary qualities that “we are not aware of their being anything other than various arrangements of the size, figure, and motions of the parts of these objects which make it possible for our nerves to move in various ways, and to excite in our soul all the various feelings which they produce there.” Similar observations were made by Newton (in Optica, 1721) about the color of rays, and by Leibnitz (in Discourse on Metaphysics, 1686) about size, figure and motion. · [src:S01:L7170-L7199](../../../INBOX/opm-libro.txt#L7170-L7199)
- **P2735** · `constraint` · This distinction was criticized by Berkeley (1710) in his “immaterialism” theory, which denied the existence of material substance altogether. · [src:S01:L7170-L7199](../../../INBOX/opm-libro.txt#L7170-L7199)
- **P2736** · `fact` · According to Berkeley, familiar objects, like a table, are only ideas in the human perceiver’s mind, and cannot exist without being perceived. · [src:S01:L7170-L7199](../../../INBOX/opm-libro.txt#L7170-L7199)
- **P2737** · `fact` · The ideas created by sensations are all that people can know for sure. · [src:S01:L7170-L7199](../../../INBOX/opm-libro.txt#L7170-L7199)
- **P2738** · `fact` · When an object is stripped of all its secondary qualities, the idea that there is some object has no support, since without qualities one cannot give any content to the idea of the object existence. · [src:S01:L7170-L7199](../../../INBOX/opm-libro.txt#L7170-L7199)
- **P2739** · `constraint` · Kant (1783) also went against this distinction, claiming that both primary and secondary, qualities are subjective, as they are located in the brain of a knowing observer. · [src:S01:L7170-L7199](../../../INBOX/opm-libro.txt#L7170-L7199)
- **P2740** · `constraint` · This discussion complements our previous treatment in Sect. 10.3 of object identity. · [src:S01:L7170-L7199](../../../INBOX/opm-libro.txt#L7170-L7199)
