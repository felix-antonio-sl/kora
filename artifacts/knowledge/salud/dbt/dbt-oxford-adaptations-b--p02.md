# The Oxford Handbook of DBT — Adaptations (Part B) - Parte 02

## DBT-PTSD: Treatment for Complex PTSD After Childhood Abuse

**Authors:** Martin Bohus, Kathlen Priebe

### Complex PTSD: Clinical Picture

Childhood sexual abuse (CSA) = nonspecific risk factor for psychopathology. New Zealand cohort: 13% of mental disorders traceable to sexual abuse. Australian cohort: CSA survivors 2.5x more likely for Axis I disorder (18.4% vs 7.0%), 5x more likely for PD (3.6% vs 0.7%). 56% of BPD patients meet co-occurring PTSD criteria, mostly CSA-related.

PTSD after CSA differs from single-incident trauma: triggers associated with everyday stimuli (proximity, intimacy, sexuality, physical care, medical visits). Dysfunctional behaviours (NSSI, suicidal ideation, aggression, substance use) conceptualized as escape strategies from intrusions/dissociation.

**cPTSD = Classic PTSD symptoms + impairments in:** emotion regulation, self-concept (inferior, worthless, unlovable, body perceived as dirty), interpersonal relationships. DSM-5 added cPTSD-related symptoms to PTSD diagnosis + dissociative subtype. ICD-11 proposes distinct cPTSD diagnosis.

### Treatment Evidence for cPTSD

General PTSD treatment meta-analyses: effect size ~1.1, remission 50-60%, ~20% premature termination. cPTSD-specific studies show lower remission rates, higher post-treatment symptoms, lower clinically significant improvement (Dorrepaal et al., 2014). Many studies excluded suicidal ideation, self-harm, substance abuse -- limiting applicability to cPTSD.

ISTSS expert survey recommended phase-oriented multimodal treatment combining emotion regulation skills and trauma-focused exposure.

### DBT-PTSD: Sources and Principles

Integrates four therapeutic frameworks:

1. **DBT** (Linehan, 1993) -- treatment framework, principles, therapeutic stance, emotion regulation skills
2. **Trauma-focused cognitive and exposure-based interventions** -- skills-assisted exposure to index trauma
3. **Compassion Focused Therapy** (Gilbert, 2010) -- self-worth, interpersonal relations
4. **Acceptance and Commitment Therapy** (Hayes, 2016) -- values clarification, acceptance of "bodyguard" thoughts, Matrix tool for valued living

**Basic assumptions:** (1) Goal = life according to client's values; (2) Trauma symptoms currently hinder goal pursuit; (3) Treatment focuses on reducing trauma-related symptoms; (4) Most symptoms maintained by escape/avoidance of primary emotions; (5) Negative self-esteem = maladaptive coping developed to survive unescapable threat; (6) Key components: skills-assisted exposure, compassion, radical acceptance, developing life worth living (in that order); (7) Highly intensive treatment by trained experts; (8) Team-based treatment.

### Treatment Phases

| Phase | Sessions (Outpatient) | Content |
|-------|----------------------|---------|
| Pre-treatment | -- | PTSD diagnosis (SCID-5, CAPS); assess crisis-generating behaviour (SBD interview); if suicide attempt <6 weeks, brief DBT first; psychoeducation; non-suicidal contract |
| 1. Commitment | 5 | Biographical/medical history, PTSD exploration, treatment orientation, previous treatment review, daily mindfulness practice (MP3 from therapist) |
| 2. Motivation & Planning | 7 | Overview of traumatic events (descriptive, not emotionally activating), values clarification, SMART goals, assess significant relationship patterns, develop individualized trauma model (trauma networks, triggers, avoidance mechanisms) |
| 3. Skills Acquisition | 4-7 | Anti-dissociation skills (balance board, ammonia, ice-packs), distress tolerance, emotion regulation for guilt/shame/anger/disgust |
| 4. Skills-Assisted Exposure | 15 | Select index trauma; in-sensu exposure with anti-dissociative skills to maintain emotional learning; 5-7 sessions per trauma memory; process secondary emotions (guilt, shame, self-contempt) with psychoeducation + metacognitive training + compassion |
| 5. Radical Acceptance | 2 | Acceptance of past facts, sequelae, current emotional responses |
| 6. Regain Your Life | 8 | Restructure life domains (partnership, sexuality, friendships, occupation); ACT Matrix for valued-living behaviour |
| 7. Farewell | 1 | Structured termination |

Total: up to 45 outpatient sessions or 12 weeks residential. Dynamic treatment hierarchy maintained throughout (life-threatening > crisis-generating > therapy-interfering).

### Skills-Assisted Exposure: Mechanism

Classic prolonged exposure contraindicated for cPTSD due to dissociation proneness. Dissociation blunts amygdala/hippocampus function, attenuating or inhibiting emotional learning. Skills-assisted exposure maintains balance between trauma activation and present awareness using anti-dissociative sensory input (loud noise, ammonia, cold temperature, eye movement, proprioceptive stimuli like balance boards).

**Primary vs secondary emotions:** Primary emotions experienced during trauma (disgust, powerlessness, threat, sexual arousal) targeted through exposure. Secondary emotions (guilt, shame, self-contempt) function as escapes from primaries -- require psychoeducation, metacognitive training, compassion-based interventions.

### Evidence

| Study | Design | N | Setting | Results |
|-------|--------|---|---------|---------|
| Steil et al., 2011 | Pre-post | 29 females | Residential, 12-week | Effect size 1.22 for PTSD symptoms; 0% dropout |
| Bohus et al., 2013 (RCT) | RCT vs TAU | 74 females | Residential | ITT between-group effect size 1.35; 5% dropout (2/36); no symptom worsening in any DBT-PTSD subject; BPD severity and prior self-harm did not predict outcome; no increase in suicidal thoughts/self-harm urges during exposure |
| Priebe et al., 2017 | Long-term follow-up | -- | Post-residential | Significant decrease in admission rates; recovery persisted at 1-year post-discharge |
| Steil et al., 2018 | Pilot | -- | Outpatient | Excellent feasibility |
| RELEASE trial (in progress) | RCT vs CPT | 206 females | Outpatient, multi-centre | cPTSD after CSA/physical abuse; 1 year each arm; no indications of high dropout or serious incidents as of 2018 |

**Secondary emotion outcomes** (residential, Gorg et al., 2017): At 3-month programme end, patients achieving healthy-control-level scores: guilt 82%, shame 67%, disgust 51%.

**Key clinical finding:** Ongoing self-harm is not a safety risk or negative predictor for DBT-PTSD outcome. No need for patients with cPTSD/BPD+PTSD to complete standard DBT before specifically designed trauma treatment.

### DBT PE vs DBT-PTSD: Comparison

| Feature | DBT PE (Harned) | DBT-PTSD (Bohus) |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| Organization | Linear stage model within standard DBT | Modular within dedicated programme |
| Prerequisite | 2 months NSSI/SI abstinence | No NSSI abstinence required (last suicide attempt >6-8 weeks) |
| Exposure type | Standard prolonged exposure procedures | Skills-assisted exposure with anti-dissociative techniques |
| Dissociation management | DBT mindfulness skills | Explicit anti-dissociation skills (sensory, proprioceptive) |
| Format | Integrated into ongoing DBT individual therapy | Standalone programme (up to 45 sessions outpatient or 12 weeks residential) |
| Additional frameworks | PE only | CFT + ACT integrated |
| Skills training | Standard DBT group continues | Individual skills teaching (no group) |
| Post-exposure | Return to standard DBT | Radical acceptance + Regain Your Life + Farewell phases |
