# The Oxford Handbook of DBT — Implementation


Chapters 37-42 covering implementation science applied to DBT, dialectical dilemmas of implementation, private practice models, national-scale rollout, international dissemination, and technology-augmented delivery.

## Implementation Science Framework for DBT

**Authors:** Katherine Anne Comtois, Sara J. Landes

Implementation science studies strategies promoting integration of research into healthcare practice. Outcome is whether research reaches policy/practice -- not whether clients improve. Proctor et al. (2011) codified eight implementation outcomes that precede service and client outcomes in a causal chain.

### Eight Implementation Outcomes

| Outcome | Definition | DBT-Specific Findings |
|---|---|---|
| Acceptability | Stakeholder agreement with DBT approach | High across studies: "reasonable," "worthwhile," "practical." Benefits for clients (symptom improvement, reduced hospitalizations) and clinicians (self-efficacy, reduced burnout, job satisfaction) |
| Appropriateness | Perceived fit to specific setting | Common concerns: 24/7 coaching impractical; DBT too costly/complex; non-behavioural orientation mismatch. Often overcome through training and discussion |
| Adoption | Decision to employ DBT | Adopted by national systems (Ireland, UK NICE, US VHA, SAMHSA). Trialability via pilot programmes increases willingness |
| Feasibility | Extent DBT can be used successfully in setting | Ditty et al. (2015): programmes endorsed 70% of developer-established elements. Key barriers: staffing/turnover (47%), lack admin support (42%), insufficient time (42%) |
| Fidelity | Extent DBT delivered as intended | Programme fidelity: PETQ, Landes Implementation Scale. Therapist fidelity: DBT Global Rating Scale (requires trained coders). More training = higher fidelity. 10-day > 2-day training for strategy use |
| Penetration | Proportion of eligible clients/providers reached | VHA range: 1%-75%, mode 1%, mean 15%. Natural clinician limit on concurrent suicidal clients constrains reach. Pilot programmes risk becoming implicit norm for low caseloads |
| Cost | Incremental implementation cost | Year of DBT comparable to prior-year standard care (Meyers et al., 2014; Priebe et al., 2012). US$6,000 decrease in client costs post-DBT. GB 36,000 savings from reduced inpatient/nursing (Amner, 2012). Cost-effectiveness depends on perspective: net savings for payers controlling all costs; minimal for outpatient providers in capitated systems |
| Sustainability | Maintenance within ongoing operations | UK review (105 programmes, 1994-2007): 63% still functioning. Deaths caused by: lack organizational support (68%), staff turnover (63%), insufficient time (56%). Two critical risk periods: year after training completion; five years after start |

### Fidelity-Feasibility Dialectic

Fidelity-focused start: few clients per therapist + intensive supervision = infeasible long-term unless client load increases post-pilot. Feasibility-focused start: partial programme or high caseloads with little supervision = sacrificed fidelity. No empirical resolution; programmes must navigate tension deliberately.

### Fidelity Measurement Tools

| Instrument | Level | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Program Elements of Treatment Questionnaire (PETQ) | Programme | Presence/absence of key DBT elements |
| Landes Implementation of DBT Scale | Programme | Detailed component assessment |
| DBT Global Rating Scale | Therapist | Adherence rating (requires trained coders) |
| DBT Certification (individual/programme) | Both | US and UK; expensive, limited uptake |

### Sustainability Strategies for Turnover

1. **External ongoing training** -- Annual training via company or academic trainer. More cost-effective for independent clinics and smaller systems. Dr. Comtois: 20-year annual university training model
2. **Train-the-trainer** -- Internal clinicians receive additional training to train others. Advantages: local perspective on barriers. Risks: "day job" conflicts; attrition (one US state: 13 trained, only 3 active after 2 years). Requires: release time, compensation to trainer's clinic, backfill
3. **Dedicated trainer position** -- Hire graduate of training into trainer role. Feasible only for larger systems; removes strong clinician from provider pool

### Key Implementation Strategies

- Introductory trainings and clinician testimonials improve acceptability/appropriateness
- Interactive trainings (discussion, Q&A, consultation) superior to didactic presentations for demonstrating setting fit
- Pilot programmes as implementation strategy: evaluate with implementation outcomes, then expand
- Focus coaching during work hours first as pragmatic entry; after-hours options emerge organically
- Target implementation outcomes before client outcomes -- analogous to DBT clinical hierarchy

## Dialectical Dilemmas of Implementation

**Authors:** Helen Best, Jim Lyng

Three signature dialectical dilemmas recur during DBT implementation. Dialectical strategies (highlighting tensions, entering paradox, making lemonade) guide resolution.

### Dilemma 1: Adopt vs. Adapt

Tension between implementing comprehensive DBT (all modes) versus partial/adapted components.

Most frequent in settings differing from original community-based university clinic (forensic, residential, inpatient). Also occurs in community services with limited resources.

Common friction points: treatment duration, provider duties/scope, modality frequency, diagnostic appropriateness, resource allocation (two group leaders), consultation team time.

**Case -- Adolescent inpatient (3-week stay):** Initial synthesis: adapted DBT with daily group skills, brief case-worker meetings (motivation function), ward-staff generalization, weekly consultation team. Evolved: added individual time with broader DBT strategies; scaled back non-DBT interventions; included family members. Improved clinical/functional outcomes.

**Case -- Young adult drop-out:** Separate 18-25 programme adopted comprehensive DBT with full fidelity. Completion rates reversed: ~75% completing full year; 70% of 35 completers fully discharged from statutory services. Greater clinically significant change than same age group in general adult programme.

### Dilemma 2: Risk Management vs. Treatment Delivery

Standard risk-management policies (hospitalization, enhanced observation, restraint) can reinforce target behaviours and block skill practice.

**Resolution approach:** Dialectical synthesis involving executive leadership, medical director, legal advisors alongside DBT team. Shift hospitalization from first-line response to last resort. Clinical assessment at point of crisis replaces automatic protocols.

**Waiting list dilemma:** Client-led stepped model -- clients choose between immediate standalone 6-month skills group (surrendering comprehensive DBT waiting list place) or wait for comprehensive programme. Highest-risk clients (medically dangerous self-harm, recent attempts) not offered standalone; actively linked with community teams while waiting.

### Dilemma 3: System Needs vs. People Needs

Tension between organizational requirements (standardized policies, budget constraints, hierarchies) and provider/client needs (flexibility, scheduling, clinical recording, phone access).

Common pressure points: overtime restrictions, union prohibitions on after-hours contact, mobile phone policies, video/audio recording permissions, consultation team time allocation.

**Case -- Community mental health centre:** Organization had fixed overtime/after-hours restrictions. Synthesis: direct billing for coaching calls up to 16 minutes; compensatory time for longer calls billed as case management. Staff used secured personal devices with commitment to time-limited, focused coaching.

### Ten Rules-of-Thumb for Implementation

1. Implementation is iterative; one training cycle will not suffice
2. Clinicians must do DBT regularly and extensively; impossible to learn with 1-2 clients/year
3. Consult widely, identify drivers, define goals before determining who to train
4. Assess contingencies -- what rewards system for tolerating risk; what rewards clinicians for commitment
5. Commit to data collection and monitoring (client outcomes, clinical performance, system evaluation)
6. Do not mandate clinicians to training or clients to treatment
7. Secure firm leadership commitment to staffing, supervision, job descriptions before starting
8. DBT is not a panacea -- effective for some clients with certain problems to varying degrees
9. Expect failure; be willing to learn from it
10. DBT requires persistence; progress can be slow but can transform services, teams, and lives

## DBT in Private Practice

**Authors:** Sarah K. Reynolds, Colleen M. Lang

### Practice Models Comparison

| Structure | Description | Benefits | Pitfalls |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equal-ownership group | One legal entity, shared business decisions | Easiest path to standard DBT; shared admin costs | Hard to dissolve; consensus difficulties; potential stuckness |
| Owner-led group | One entity, owner makes structural decisions | Easier decision-making; standard DBT in one location | Employer-employee tensions; owners may drift from clinical work; clinician burnout if limits unobserved |
| Co-located sole proprietors | Separate business entities sharing space for consultation team/clients | Clinical collaboration without business entanglement; shared space costs | May be difficult to find compatible clinician |
| Distributed sole proprietors | Separate locations; commute for consultation team | Maximum flexibility; works in rural settings; classic model | Most adaptation needed; team commitment fragile; skills acquisition challenging |

### Functions vs. Modes Principle

Solo practitioners may not offer standard DBT modes but must articulate how each of five treatment functions is served:
1. Enhance client motivation
2. Enhance client capabilities
3. Ensure generalization
4. Enhance therapist motivation/skills (consultation team)
5. Structure treatment environment

### Business-Service Dialectic

DBT selects for altruistic practitioners prone to negative judgments about earning profit. Polarization risk: excessive altruism (not targeting non-payment, accepting too many clients, waiving fees reflexively) vs. excessive business focus (over-scheduling, dropping unreimbursed modes, customer-pleasing at expense of treatment fidelity).

**Antidotes:**
- Self-assessment: identify personal polarization patterns using consultation team
- A priori financial plan: determine net income needed, add business expenses, divide by working weeks, calculate minimum weekly caseload at set fee
- Written financial policy statement given at intake covering fees, payment timing, cancellation policy, insurance

### Implementation Decisions

**Client selection:** Full-time solo practitioners typically treat DBT clients as 35-75% of overall caseload (20-30 total clients). Stage 1 intake at intervals (4-8 weeks) for stabilization. Direct phone screening (15-30 min) before scheduling intake to assess logistical and clinical fit.

**Fee management:** Session fee must offset substantial unreimbursed time (consultation team, coaching, case management). Options: bundled weekly rate via insurance; package rate for combined individual + skills group; reduced fee for hard-to-fill time slots; shorter/more frequent sessions; trainee at reduced cost. Non-payment targeted as TIB.

**Consultation team:** Required for private practice -- absence = partial implementation. 60-75 min weekly with 4-6 members typical. Choose members for DBT skill, capacity for dialectical thinking, comfort with direct feedback. Supplement shorter weekly meetings with monthly extended sessions for case conceptualization.

**Skills group:** Solo leader feasible with small groups (5-7 members), careful screening, pre-treatment orientation/commitment. Minimum 3 clients to start (4 preferred). Cross-referral between team members' groups ideal. Allow shorter minimum commitment (one module) if needed to recruit.

**Coaching:** Private practice removes institutional after-hours restrictions. Options: single phone for work/personal, separate phone with Google Voice, SMS. Clarify limits (response time, nighttime availability).

**Pre-treatment phase:** Orient to all treatment modes, financial policies, contingencies. Seek commitment while maintaining non-attached stance. Define minimum treatment interval (6 months). Target potential TIBs proactively. Avoid fast-forwarding through commitment out of customer-service pressure.

## National Implementation: Ireland's DBT Project

**Authors:** Daniel M. Flynn, Mary Kells, Mary Joyce

### Context and Drivers

Ireland BPD prevalence: 11-20% of outpatient presentations. Three policy catalysts:
1. **National Self-Harm Registry Ireland** (2001): documented high rates, generated clinical/political attention
2. **Decriminalization of suicide** (1993): enabled recording of suicide deaths; revealed high mortality rates
3. **"A Vision for Change" report** (2006): recommended dedicated DBT team per catchment area (300,000 population) with staff seconded 1.5 days/week from community mental health teams

### Local Pilot: North Lee Adult Mental Health Service

Team self-funded UK training (2010). Multidisciplinary composition: Psychology, Nursing, Psychiatry, Art Therapy. Results (n=12 completers):

| Metric | 12 months pre-DBT | During DBT | 12 months post-DBT |
|---|---|---|---|
| ED visits | 49 | 6 | 7 |
| Inpatient admissions | 12 | 3 | 1 |
| Bed days | 207 | 45 | 1 |
| Cost | EUR 144,900 | EUR 31,500 | EUR 700 |

Significant reductions in borderline symptoms, suicidal ideation, depression, hopelessness. Increased quality of life (psychological health). Gains maintained at 6-month follow-up.

### Scaling: Local to National

1. Positive pilot data persuaded National Office for Suicide Prevention (NOSP) to fund Cork-region expansion (182,000 to 520,000 population coverage)
2. Individual teams submitting ad hoc funding requests to NOSP revealed need for coordinated approach
3. Clinical leads drafted proposal: train 16 teams across Ireland over two years with comprehensive evaluation
4. **National DBT Project** established with NOSP funding

### Implementation Framework

Used Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (Damschroder et al., 2009). Fifth construct (Process) most relevant:

1. **Planning:** Appointed coordinating team (coordinator, administrator, research team). Established National Steering Group Committee (mental health experts, management, DBT experts, researchers, policy developers, service user, family representative). Application process requiring area management sign-off and 1.5 days/week commitment per staff member
2. **Engaging:** Mandatory management meetings with clinical leads before training. Team orientation meetings. Commitment-strengthening meeting with treatment developer (Linehan), Minister for Mental Health, NOSP Director, local managers, service users, families
3. **Executing:** Intensive Training Part I, followed by programme implementation, then Part II within 8 months. Post-training contact to encourage implementation
4. **Reflecting/Evaluating:** Three-arm evaluation: effectiveness, implementation process, economic analysis. Quantitative and qualitative feedback from service users and therapists

### Barriers and Solutions

| Barrier | Solution |
|---|---|
| Absence of management support | Education meetings with management; commitment signatures; local champion engagement |
| Funding difficulties | National funding secured for training + supervision; coordinating team appointed |
| Insufficient resources | Training contingent on management agreement to minimum commitment; additional resources contingent on ongoing productivity |
| Staff turnover | Annual additional training places for established teams; application-based replacement process |
| Lack of population-level view | Steering Group Committee prioritized teams based on population need and registry data |

### Outcomes

16 teams trained (9 adult, 7 child/adolescent); 124 therapists. All 16 teams still functioning at 2.5 years (Cohort 1) and 1.7 years (Cohort 2) post-training. Staff turnover managed through replacement training.

### Selection Criteria for Teams

1. Evidence of need (registry data, therapist reports, availability of existing DBT)
2. Evidence of team commitment (application, phone calls, orientation meeting)
3. Commitment from local management (signatures, conference call)
4. Evidence of evaluation/research commitment
5. Meeting training provider requirements
6. Sample characteristics aligned with evidence base

### Key Lessons

- Innovation champion essential: must understand both treatment model and organizational/political culture
- System readiness prerequisite: champion must recognize when system is primed for change
- Ongoing commitment management: monitor, review, provide evidence, collaborate with stakeholders
- Coordinating team as facilitator: anticipates barriers, connects teams to resources, provides support
- National mandate still lacking: coverage remains incomplete where local management does not support

## International Implementation and Cross-Cultural Training

**Authors:** Anthony P. DuBose, Yevgeny Botanov, Andre Ivanoff

### Global Need

- ~1 million suicide deaths annually (WHO, 2014); third leading cause of death ages 15-44
- Conservative estimate: 3% of world population (~135 million people) will experience severe emotion dysregulation requiring treatment
- 35+ RCTs published across 10 countries. DBT publications: 8/year (1993-2000), 41/year (2001-2010), 78/year (2011+)

### Dissemination and Implementation Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Diffusion | Passive, untargeted spread of new ideas |
| Dissemination | Active, planned spread via determined channels |
| Implementation | Process of integrating evidence-based intervention into targeted setting |

### Implementation History

DBT spread through two primary mechanisms: (1) international providers travelling to US for Intensive Training, then disseminating locally; (2) US trainers travelling to interested countries. First non-US programmes: UK, Ireland, New Zealand, Canada, Germany, Netherlands, Scandinavia -- all within a decade of 1993 publication.

Current reach: ~30 countries across six continents. RCTs conducted in Canada, Netherlands, Australia, Great Britain, Germany, Norway, Spain, Sweden, Denmark, New Zealand, Egypt, Taiwan.

### What Is Being Implemented

**Programme elements:** Four modes serving five functions. Study of 52 US-trained teams: 75% implemented all four modes within one year; 86% at least three modes (Harned et al., 2016).

**Provider behaviours:** Core strategies, specific procedures, special treatment strategies, treatment protocols. DBT is principle-based (not protocol-driven): requires deeper understanding than manualized treatments. Cannot use lay-therapist models designed for simpler interventions.

### Considerations for International Implementation

| Factor | Key Issues |
|---|---|
| Technology | Video/audio platforms; internet bandwidth; secure file sharing; cloud services for materials (shipping least effective) |
| Working in foreign country | Work visas (distinct from tourism/conference); taxation; currency agreements; exchange rate fluctuations; jurisdiction for disputes; cultural sensitivity in contract language |
| Language/translation | Three arenas: training materials, verbal presentations, patient materials. Standards: expert concurrence + back-translation of selected samples. Translations take ~2 years. Glossary of DBT-specific terms essential for simultaneous interpretation |
| Culture | Idiographic assessment accommodates cultural variation. Cross-cultural considerations: communication styles, spiritual practices, system-level discrimination, family engagement norms, mental health attitudes. RCTs across six continents confirm cultural adaptability |
| Healthcare system variability | Public funding limits (sometimes inpatient only); biologically-driven psychiatry excluding psychosocial interventions; BPD not recognized in some countries; scope-of-practice restrictions (only psychiatrists treat suicidal patients in some jurisdictions); absence of private/public mental health funding |

### Training Models

- **Online training** and in-person workshops superior to manual reading for DBT knowledge acquisition (Dimeff et al., 2009, 2011, 2015)
- **Instructor-led training:** Higher satisfaction; critical for establishing initial leadership team and regional foothold
- **Trainer development (Behavioral Tech model):** Competency-based, not time-based. Factors affecting speed: learning rate, behaviour therapy background, patient volume/variety, setting diversity, mentorship availability, opportunities to demonstrate training competencies

### Long-Term Viability Factors

1. Early identification of local leadership and future in-country trainers
2. In-country hosts for venue, marketing, registration, administration
3. Internal mentor teams from initial training cohorts guiding subsequent trainees
4. Independent in-country organization to sustain long-term D&I
5. Linehan Institute affiliation levels: individual dissemination champions to independent training organizations
