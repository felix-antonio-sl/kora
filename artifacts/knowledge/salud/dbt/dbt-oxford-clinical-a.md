# The Oxford Handbook of DBT — Clinical Applications (Part A)


## Case Formulation: Behavioural Analysis and Treatment Planning

DBT case conceptualization integrates assessment, formulation, and intervention into a single evolving plan. Requirements: clear, concise, operationally specific, non-judgmental language; generalizable across behavioural patterns; empirically grounded; useful for treatment planning.

Temporal scope ranges from case-based to episode-based to moment-based. Formulations evolve throughout treatment via session-by-session quantitative monitoring.

### Case Conceptualization Template

1. Demographics (diagnosis, treatment history, referral reason)
2. Initial assessment: five areas of dysregulation + strengths + deficits
3. Biosocial theory formulation
4. Stage of treatment
5. Life Worth Living Goals (LWLGs)
6. Behaviourally defined problems (increase/decrease)
7. Primary target hierarchy (all stages)
8. Secondary targets
9. Hypotheses of controlling variables
10. Solution analysis plan
11. Predicted outcomes and measurement

### Five Areas of Dysregulation

| Area | Core Feature | Assessment Focus |
|------|-------------|-----------------|
| Emotional | Over/under-control of emotions | Role of shame, guilt, sadness, anger; SUDs ratings |
| Behavioural | Consequence of or attempt to regulate emotion | Self-harm urges, cutting, substance use linked to emotional states |
| Interpersonal | Chaotic, unpredictable relationships | Fluctuating emotions destabilize relationship maintenance |
| Self | Identity disturbance | Impaired self-examination under high arousal; unclear likes, values, goals |
| Cognitive | Attentional/perceptual disturbance | Dissociation, paranoia, brief psychosis, concentration problems |

Assessment also evaluates strengths (social support, family, finances, housing, intellectual capacity, vocational/educational abilities, leisure, religious community, reasons for living, existing skills) and deficits/barriers to change.

### Biosocial Theory of Aetiology

Pervasive emotion dysregulation arises from emotional vulnerability (high sensitivity + high reactivity + slow return to baseline) transacting over time with an invalidating environment.

Three invalidating environmental responses:
1. Criticizing, pathologizing, or negating private experience communications
2. Intermittently reinforcing escalated emotional displays while extinguishing modulated expression
3. Communicating that life problems are simple to solve

Assessment purpose: identify current behavioural patterns resulting from this transaction.

### Stages of Treatment

| Stage | Focus | Modes | Targets |
|-------|-------|-------|---------|
| Stage 1 | Behavioural stabilization | Individual therapy + skills group + phone coaching | Life-threatening behaviour, therapy-interfering behaviour, quality-of-life-interfering behaviour, skills acquisition |
| Stage 2 | Emotional processing | Primarily exposure protocols | Decrease experiential avoidance, increase emotional experiencing, decrease emptiness, decrease non-belonging |
| Stage 3 | Ordinary life problems | Single-problem targeting; evidence-based protocols where available | Vocational issues, depression, relationship issues, skills generalization |

### Life Worth Living Goals

LWLGs are the foundational anchor for all treatment. They describe activities, people, jobs, events making life full and complete --- not therapy goals. All problems evaluated against interference with LWLGs; all new behaviours assessed for movement toward/away from LWLGs.

Therapist does not initially judge realism of goals. Wise mind process helps client refine goals over time. LWLGs revisited throughout treatment.

### Road Map: From Goals to Treatment

1. Determine LWLGs
2. Translate LWLGs into behaviourally defined problems (increase/decrease)
3. Translate problems into primary target hierarchy
4. Get commitment to target problems
5. Select targets for diary card monitoring
6. Conduct thorough behavioural assessment (frequency, intensity, topography, duration)
7. Conduct chain analyses on occurrences; identify controlling variables; generate and implement solutions
8. After several chains, generate hypothesis about controlling variable and test
9. Begin treatment addressing controlling variables (contingency management, cognitive modification, exposure, behavioural skills)
10. Update diary card to reflect refinement
11. Repeat for all target behaviours until LWLGs achieved

### Primary Target Hierarchy (Stage 1)

Targets ordered by priority --- session agenda follows this hierarchy:

1. **Life-threatening behaviours (LTBs):** Suicide (urges, actions, ideation), self-harm, homicide, assault
2. **Therapy-interfering behaviours (TIBs):** Non-compliant, non-collaborative, non-attending behaviours (by client or therapist)
3. **Quality-of-life-interfering behaviours (QOL):** Substance use, depression, medical disorders, bipolar behaviours, gambling, binging/purging, avoidance, premature quitting of jobs/relationships
4. **Behavioural skills (increase):** Core mindfulness, interpersonal effectiveness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance

### Secondary Targets: Dialectical Dilemmas

Three behavioural continuums; when one pole identified, its opposite typically also present:

| Pole A | Pole B | Clinical Manifestation |
|--------|--------|----------------------|
| Emotional vulnerability | Self-invalidation | "I can't take it" vs. "I'm a bad person"; avoidance of all emotion vs. unrealistic self-expectations |
| Active passivity | Apparent competence | Actively eliciting help/doing nothing vs. masking distress ("I'm fine"); environment over- or under-responds |
| Unrelenting crisis | Inhibited grieving | Problematic escape behaviours compounding crises vs. shutting down to avoid painful emotions |

### Controlling Variables and Hypothesis Generation

Chain analyses across multiple instances of target behaviours yield hypotheses about controlling variables:

- **Contextual controlling variables:** vulnerability factors, prompting events, links (cognitive, emotion, action, sensation, events)
- **Consequential controlling variables:** proximal/distal internal consequences, proximal/distal environmental consequences
- **Function:** respondent (cued by antecedents), operant (maintained by consequences), or both

After treating several behaviours, cross-behavioural patterns (insight analysis) emerge. Shared controlling variables across behaviours become priority treatment targets.

### Solution Analysis: Matching Interventions to Chain Position

| Chain Position | Intervention Type | Examples |
|---------------|-------------------|----------|
| Vulnerability factors | Problem solving, behavioural skills | Remove lethal means; PLEASE for sleep; crisis survival skills |
| Prompting events | Problem solving, behavioural skills | Decrease conflict; DEAR MAN GIVE |
| Cognitive links | Cognitive modification | Change "I am a burden"; dialectical thinking; decrease rumination |
| Emotion links | Exposure | Classically conditioned shame, sadness |
| Action/event links | Problem solving | Stimulus control; managing others' behaviours |
| Any link | Behavioural skills | Mindfulness, distress tolerance, interpersonal effectiveness, emotion regulation |
| Consequences (proximal/distal) | Behavioural skills, cognitive modification, contingency management | Replacement behaviours; contingency clarification; shaping/reinforcing new behaviours; extinguishing old behaviours |
| After-effects | Behavioural skills, exposure, problem solving | Repair guilt; ABC PLEASE; shame exposure in session; review/remove means |

### Outcome Measurement

Outcomes tracked via standardized instruments (BSL-23, DERS) and diary card behaviours. Evaluated monthly, quarterly, by skills module, or by treatment contract. When agreed-upon thresholds met, treatment advances to next behaviour or stage.

## Behavioural Chain Analysis and Solution Analysis

Behavioural chain analysis (BCA) is the primary functional assessment tool in DBT. Conducted nearly every session on single specific instances of target behaviours.

### Functions of Chain Analysis

1. **Understanding:** Detailed sequence challenges belief that events occur "out of the blue"
2. **Hypothesis testing:** Empirical method to test theories about controlling variables across multiple instances
3. **Validation:** Client observes causes of behaviour, reducing self-blame and judgmental thoughts
4. **Solution generation:** Identifies intervention points for preventing future occurrences

### Components of a Complete Chain Analysis

1. **Problem behaviour** --- single specific instance; client's own behaviour; described topographically (not globally)
2. **Prompting event** --- external stimulus that triggered the behaviour; may be unknown (hypothesize)
3. **Consequences** --- short-term (maintaining/reinforcing) and long-term (motivation for change)
4. **Links** --- actions, events, thoughts, emotions, physical sensations, urges between prompting event and behaviour; maintained in chronological order
5. **Vulnerability factors** --- acute (tired, hungry, recent fight) or chronic (strained relationship, chronic pain, medication non-adherence)

### Conducting First BCA: Recommended Sequence

For new clients, use non-chronological teaching order:
1. Define problem behaviour precisely (topography, frequency, intensity, duration)
2. Identify prompting event
3. Identify consequences (short-term and long-term)
4. Fill in links between prompting event and problem behaviour ("slow motion" replay)
5. Identify vulnerability factors

Once familiar with structure, revert to chronological order.

### Behavioural Description Standards

When clients use vague terms, obtain specifics through:
- **Frequency:** "How many times did you have that thought?"
- **Duration:** "How long were you angry?"
- **Intensity:** "On a scale from 0--100, how angry were you?"

### Intervening at Each Chain Component

| Component | Intervention Strategies |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Vulnerability factors | PLEASE skills; interpersonal skills (DEAR MAN, GIVE); target later in treatment |
| Prompting events | Stimulus control (with care: avoid inadvertent avoidance learning); exposure; create new prompting events for positive alternatives |
| Thought links | Thought records, cognitive restructuring, non-judgmental stance |
| Emotion/sensation links | Mindfulness of emotion, opposite action, TIP |
| Urge links | Urge surfing (ride the wave until it dissipates); noticing urges as choice points |
| Action links | Skills training, behavioural rehearsal |
| Consequences | Client self-reinforcement; significant others modify responses; therapist as consequence source (reinforce effective behaviour, matter-of-fact response to suicidal communications) |

### Solution Analysis Procedure

1. Review completed chain with client --- highlight multiple intervention opportunities (generates hope)
2. Client selects link to target
3. Brainstorm solutions (minimum ~10; include creative/outrageous options to encourage flexibility)
4. Client picks two best options based on: ease of implementation, likely effectiveness, willingness to do
5. Troubleshoot obstacles: skills available? access to needed items? likelihood of follow-through? who could help?
6. Assign chosen solution as homework; commit to specific day/time
7. Follow up on homework next session (failure to follow up decreases future compliance)

### Identifying Common Links Across Chains

Lay out multiple chains (or use a comparison table) to identify recurring elements across behaviours. Common links are high-yield intervention targets ("more bang for the buck"). Common elements include: recurring thought patterns, shared emotions, similar consequences, vulnerability-to-consequence feedback loops.

### Practical Considerations

- **Briefer chains** (5 min) appropriate when: client experienced with BCA; therapist already knows common links; behaviour is not primary target
- **Outside-session chains:** Client completes BCA soon after behaviour occurs (better accuracy). Teaches self-therapy skill for post-treatment
- **Shame management:** Use non-judgmental, factual descriptions. Check in on client's emotions during chain. Analyse success experiences to balance aversiveness
- **Memory difficulties:** Educate about stress/arousal effects on memory. Provide contextual prompts ("What did you do that morning?"). Hypothesize for gaps. Memory improves with practice
- **"I don't know" responses:** Offer hypotheses; assign self-monitoring homework to test hypotheses in next similar situation

## Emotion Regulation: Conceptual Framework and Clinical Practice

Emotion regulation is central to DBT. Linehan conceptualized problem behaviours (self-harm, suicide attempts) as consequences of dysregulated emotions or direct attempts to avoid/reduce aversive emotional sensations. An entire skills training module addresses emotion regulation (acquisition phase), but strengthening and generalization must occur through individual therapy and phone coaching.

## Dangers of Over-Coaching Distress Tolerance

Therapists frequently over-rely on distress tolerance skills during chain/solution analysis and phone coaching. Distraction-based distress tolerance is analogous to treating a broken leg with analgesic: client learns new avoidance methods but fails to gain mastery over experiencing emotions.

Signs of over-reliance on distress tolerance:
1. Client reports "skills don't work"
2. No change in circumstances; no movement toward life worth living
3. Distress increasing rather than reducing
4. Seeking more body-numbing solutions (prescription drugs, alcohol, food restriction, excessive sleep)

When these signs persist, suspect: client failing to solve legitimate problems (accommodation, employment, relationships, finances, legal, health) and not recognizing that accompanying emotions are justified.

## Emotion Theory: Functions and Action Urges

Each emotion evolved for a specific situation and prompts a particular adaptive action. The emotion "fits the facts" when the situation matches the evolutionary context.

| Emotion | Fits the Facts When... | Function | Action Urge |
|---------|----------------------|----------|-------------|
| Anger | Blocked in goal pursuit or threatened | Energy to overcome obstacle or fight threat | Attack |
| Sadness | Loss occurred | (1) Conserve resources; (2) mobilize help | (1) Withdraw; (2) seek/pine for lost item |
| Fear | Life/health in danger | Preserve life by avoiding danger | (1) Avoid/run; (2) freeze if danger too close |
| Joy | Activity benefits health/quality of life | Maximize gains | Do more of what set off joy |
| Shame | Action risks group expulsion | Minimize expulsion likelihood | Hide self or misdemeanour |
| Guilt | Action transgresses group norms | Maximize chances of remaining in group | Make reparation |
| Envy | Another has desired advantage | Reduce discrepancy | Acquire or destroy coveted thing |
| Jealousy | Risk another may take/destroy possession | Keep precious things safe | Guard jealously; warn off rivals |
| Disgust | Danger of toxic contamination | Prevent harm from noxious association | Recoil from or repel contaminant |
| Interest | Stimulus with potential | Expand knowledge range | Attend to, explore, pursue |
| Surprise | Stimulus contrary to expectations | Allow adjustment to change | (1) Stop; (2) reappraise and assimilate |

## Unique Signature of Each Emotion

Each emotion has a distinct combination of features across eight domains: temperature, facial expression, breathing, posture, gesture, muscle tone, voice tone, actions in environment.

Two-way maintenance cycle between domains and emotion intensity: coaching client to act opposite to the emotion-specific signature in each domain reduces that emotion's intensity incrementally.

| Emotion | Temp | Facial Expression | Breathing | Posture | Muscles | Voice |
|---------|------|-------------------|-----------|---------|---------|-------|
| Anger | Hot | Scrunched brow, clenched jaw, narrowed eyes | Quick, shallow, huffing | Rigid, squaring up | Tense, clenched | Raised, accusatory |
| Sadness | Cold | Downcast eyes, mouth down, crying | Long sighs, sobs | Loose, sagging, curled | Floppy, no tension | Low, slow, breathy |
| Fear | Cold | Wide eyes, open mouth pulled down | Holding or rapid shallow | Escape-ready, tense forward | Tense, trembling | Silent or hushed |
| Anxiety | Hot | Darting eyes, furrowed brow | Rapid, shallow | Defensive, making self smaller | Tense | Intense staccato |
| Joy | Warm | Open, smiling, raised eyebrows | Regular | Expansive, elongated | Fluid with tone | Light, musical |
| Shame | Hot | Gaze diagonal down, no eye contact | Slow, upper chest | Head lowered, curling up | Droopy, tensing on approach | Absent |
| Guilt | Warm | Head tilted down, eyes up | Slower, upper chest | Slightly shrugged | Moderate tension | Restricted, lower, urgent |
| Disgust | Cool | Curled upper lip, scrunched nose | Breath holding, exaggerated exhale | Twisted, asymmetric | Tensed stomach, gag reflex | Scathing, sneering |

## Three Essential Principles for Emotion Regulation

1. **Only one emotion at a time.** Clumping emotions together overwhelms client and prevents down-regulation. Facial expression clues identify dominant emotion.
2. **Regulate to appropriate level, not elimination.** Emotion "unwarranted by degree" requires adjustment, not eradication. Too much down-regulation invalidates the warranted portion.
3. **One size does not fit all.** Same physical action may de-intensify one emotion while intensifying another (e.g., lowered shoulders reduces anger but intensifies shame). Do not confuse de-arousal for emotion regulation.

## Discerning Appropriate Emotional Intensity

Emotion regulation = thermostat adjustment, not on/off switch. Client must determine:
1. How much emotion is currently experienced? (0--100 scale)
2. How much is valid for this situation?

Procedure for calibrating intensity:
1. Acknowledge emotion fits facts to some degree (validating)
2. Client uses wise mind to determine appropriate level --- therapist does not prescribe
3. Compare current intensity to appropriate level
4. Down-regulate (or up-regulate) to target level
5. Problem-solve the warranted portion

## Acting Opposite in Each Domain

Down-regulation procedure (anger example):
1. **Temperature:** Cool down (open window, cold drink, cold flannel on wrists/neck)
2. **Facial expression:** Relax facial muscles, unclench jaw, smooth forehead
3. **Posture:** Lean back, drop shoulders
4. **Breathing:** Lengthen outbreath, slow and deepen
5. **Gesture:** Inhibit aggressive gestures
6. **Muscle tone:** Progressive release of tension
7. **Voice tone:** Lower volume and pitch, slower pace (including internal self-talk)
8. **Actions in environment:** Opposite of emotion-driven actions (move slowly vs. stomp)

Each domain addressed opposite reduces intensity by incremental degrees. Client evaluates effect after each domain, adjusts, re-employs.

## Shaping Protocol for Emotion Regulation Skills

1. Employ the skill
2. Evaluate effect
3. Adjust approach
4. Re-employ (re-evaluate, re-adjust)
5. Continue until close to desired result
