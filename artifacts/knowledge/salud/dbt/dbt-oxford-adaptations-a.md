# The Oxford Handbook of DBT — Adaptations (Part A)

Compressed reference covering six adapted applications of DBT: adolescents, eating disorders, substance use disorders, forensic settings, inpatient settings, and college counselling centres. Source: Swales (ed.), *The Oxford Handbook of Dialectical Behaviour Therapy*, 2018, Chapters 25-30.

## DBT for Adolescents

Authors: Jill H. Rathus, Alec L. Miller, Lauren Bonavitacola.

### Rationale and Target Population

DBT-A targets suicidal, self-injuring, multi-problem adolescents with emotional and behavioural dysregulation. Applicable across severity continuum: from light social drinking and first signs of self-harm to severe suicidality, NSSI, disordered eating, substance use, risky sexual behaviour. DBT views all problem behaviours as consequences of emotional dysregulation or attempts to re-regulate intense emotions.

### Key Adaptations from Standard DBT

| Domain | Standard Adult DBT | DBT-A Adaptation | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skills group format | Individual adults | Multi-family group (teens + caregivers together) | Increase understanding/skill in home environment; multiple families reduce drift to single-family problem-solving |
| Treatment commitment | 12 months | 6 months | Developmental appropriateness; sufficient for Stage 1 |
| Skills modules | 4 modules | 5 modules (added Walking the Middle Path) | Address teen-family dialectical dilemmas, polarized conflict, invalidation |
| Phone coaching | Client only | Both adolescents and parents | Parents lack own individual therapist to call |
| Family sessions | Not standard mode | As-needed family + parenting sessions | Address familial contingencies reinforcing maladaptive behaviour |
| Environmental intervention | Minimal (consultation-to-patient preferred) | Increased | Teens have less power with adult authority figures |
| Skills language | Adult level | Simplified language, added graphics, teen-relevant examples | Developmental accessibility |
| QoL targets | Adult issues | School-related, peer, social media, identity confusion | Age-appropriate |

### Walking the Middle Path Module

Fifth skills module unique to DBT-A. Three components: dialectics and teen-family dialectical dilemmas, validation skills, behaviour change principles. Treatment acceptability rated 4.23/5 by teens and parents. Validation rated most helpful skill across both adolescents and caregivers.

Three adolescent-family dialectical dilemmas with corresponding secondary targets:

| Dilemma | Increase | Decrease |
|---|---|---|
| Excessive leniency vs. authoritarian control | Authoritative discipline; adolescent self-determination | Excessive leniency; authoritarian control |
| Normalizing pathological vs. pathologizing normative behaviours | Recognition of normative behaviours; identification of pathological behaviours | Pathologizing normative; normalizing pathological |
| Forcing autonomy vs. fostering dependence | Individuation; effective reliance on others | Excessive dependence; excessive autonomy |

### DBT-A Treatment Modes

- Multi-family skills training group (24 weeks, 5 modules)
- Individual DBT therapy
- Phone coaching for teens and family members
- Family sessions (as needed)
- Parenting sessions (as needed)
- Therapist consultation team
- Possible ancillary: pharmacotherapy, therapeutic/residential schools

### Biosocial Theory Application to Adolescents

Biological vulnerability: emotional sensitivity, high reactivity, slow return to baseline. Preliminary neurobiological evidence: decreased left ACC volume and decreased ACG volume in adolescent BPD (Whittle et al., 2009; Goodman et al., 2011). No fMRI amygdala studies in adolescent BPD as of 2018.

Invalidating environments for teens include peers, teachers, school personnel, social media provocations, bullying, social exclusion, LGBTQ experiences of hetero-normative culture. Transaction is bidirectional: high vulnerability + normal invalidation, or low vulnerability + severe invalidation, both yield dysregulation.

### Confidentiality Management

Parent may share information with therapist (teen informed); therapist does not share confidential teen information with parent. Parents urged to copy teen on emails. Therapist protects ongoing low-level risk behaviour as confidential. Escalating or imminent risk: parents informed, ideally with teen present and participating.

### Evidence Base

**Quasi-experimental studies:** Feasibility and promising outcomes for reducing target behaviours in suicidal multi-problem youth (Fleischhaker et al., 2011; Katz et al., 2004; Rathus & Miller, 2002).

**Open trials (18+):** Multiple diagnoses with suicidality/NSSI, BPD, externalizing disorders (forensic and outpatient), eating disorders (BN, BED, AN), school refusal, residential treatment, long-term inpatient, school settings, chronic medical non-compliance.

**RCTs:**

| Study | Design | N | Duration | Results |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mehlum et al. (2014) | DBT vs. EUC, Norway | 77 | 16 weeks | DBT superior: reduced self-harm, suicidal ideation, depression, BPD symptoms. Large effect sizes for DBT vs. small for EUC. 1-year follow-up: sustained self-harm reduction |
| Goldstein et al. (2015) | DBT vs. TAU, bipolar adolescents | 20 (14 DBT, 6 TAU) | 12 months | DBT: greater depression reductions, trends toward reduced suicidal ideation and emotion dysregulation |
| McCauley et al. (2016) | CARES multi-site RCT, DBT vs. Supportive Therapy | Large | 6 months | Sharp decline in suicide attempts; significantly lower rate at end of treatment vs. control; reduced self-harm and suicidal ideation |

**Cultural adaptations:** Suicidal Latinas (German et al., 2015); substance-using Native Americans/Alaska Natives (Beckstead et al., 2015).

### Future Directions

Pre-adolescent children adaptation (Perepletchikova et al., 2017): further simplified handouts, simplified chain analyses, large parent training component. School-based prevention: STEPS-A curriculum (Mazza et al., 2016) for general education classrooms. Early detection/prevention of BPD via Global Alliance for Prevention and Early Intervention (GAP).

## DBT for Eating Disorders

Authors: Kelly A. C. Bhatnagar, Caitlin Martin-Wagar, Lucene Wisniewski.

### Rationale

Eating pathology (restriction, purging, binge-eating) functions as emotion regulation mechanism. DBT addresses affect regulation transdiagnostically. Specific advantages: motivational strategies for ego-syntonic AN symptoms; acceptance-based strategies for ambivalence; strategies targeting premature drop-out (ED drop-out rates: 20.2-70%); non-judgmental stance counters negative attributions; consultation team provides "therapy for therapists" managing burnout; consultation-to-patient develops mastery/self-efficacy; addresses high ED-BPD comorbidity (3% AN, 21% BN).

### Two Treatment Pathways

| Feature | Adapted DBT for ED | Comprehensive DBT for ED |
|---|---|---|
| Target population | Primary, uncomplicated BED or BN | Complex, comorbid ED (failed CBT/FBT, co-occurring BPD, SUD, chronic suicidality, emotion regulation deficits) |
| Duration | 6 months skills training | Standard 12-month comprehensive |
| Modes | Skills group (group for BED, individual for BN) | All standard DBT modes |
| Skills taught | All except Interpersonal Effectiveness | All four modules unedited |
| Manual | Safer, Telch, & Chen (2009) | Standard DBT manuals |

### Eligibility Criteria for Comprehensive DBT-ED

(A) Meets ED diagnostic criteria + medically stable for outpatient. (B) Documented emotion regulation difficulties (clinically significant DERS score). (C) Two or more DBT-evidence symptoms (self-harm, suicidality, impulsivity, substance abuse, affective instability, interpersonal disturbance). For patients already in CBT/FBT: (D) complicating comorbidities, (E) slow treatment response, (F) therapy-interfering behaviours obstructing manualized agenda.

### Target Hierarchy Modifications

| Target Level | Standard DBT | DBT-ED Adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| Life-threatening (I) | Suicide, NSSI | + ED behaviours during medical instability (vomiting while hypokalaemic, exercising while bradycardic, water-loading while hyponatraemic) |
| Therapy-interfering (II) | Non-compliance, lateness | + Cognitive impairment from malnutrition (non-attention), water-loading before weigh-ins |
| Quality-of-life (III) | Various | ED behaviours outside medical instability |

### Skills Training Modifications

All four modules taught unedited, but specific safety considerations:

- **TIP skills contraindicated** for patients with bradycardia history (temperature change, intense exercise, progressive relaxation) unless medically cleared
- **Self-soothe with taste** acknowledged as overused/ED-reinforcing; teach broader skills range
- **Mindful eating:** Increases negative affect in AN/BN patients (Marek et al., 2013). Distraction more effective for restricting/purging patients with high behavioural dyscontrol. Mindful eating helpful for BED (reduces binge/overeating, increases internal awareness)
- **PLEASE skills:** Exercise component requires medical clearance

### Phone Coaching Modifications

- Standard 24-hour rule adapted to **Next Meal/Snack Rule**: after ED behaviour, patient cannot call until next scheduled meal/snack; previous ED behaviour not addressed on coaching call (reserved for next session)
- **Accountability calls:** Patient schedules pre-meal coaching call for support with avoidant eating behaviours

### Diary Card Adaptation

Monitors food/beverage consumption, time of day, location, planned vs. actual meals, hunger scale, food groups tracked (protein, vegetable, grain, lipid, fruit, dairy). Urges/behaviours tracked: restriction, binge-eating, vomiting, laxative/diuretic/diet pill use, over-exercise, body dissatisfaction, urge to quit treatment/recovery, lying/withholding. Medication compliance tracked. Medical protocols noted (bradycardia, tachycardia, orthostasis).

### Adolescent ED: Blended FBT/DBT Model

For youth with EDs: most DBT-A adaptations apply directly. Key variation: regular family therapy via FBT (not just "as needed"). Rationale: FBT has strong evidence base as gold standard for adolescent ED; narrow window (~3 years) for early intervention before chronicity. Blended model: DBT individual therapist (versed in FBT) targets life-threatening/therapy-interfering/QoL symptoms + FBT family therapist (versed in DBT) focuses on empowering parents to manage ED symptoms.

### Evidence Base

**BED:** Reduced binge-eating and ED pathology (Telch et al., 2000, 2001; Safer et al., 2001b, 2010; Masson et al., 2013). Drop-out 0-28%.

**BN:** Reduced BN symptoms (Hill et al., 2011; Safer et al., 2001a, 2001b). Low premature drop-out (13.3% in case series).

**AN:** Preliminary: increased BMI, reduced ED psychopathology (Chen et al., 2015; Lynch et al., 2013 RO-DBT drop-out 27.7%).

**Transdiagnostic/multidiagnostic:** Significant support (Chen et al., 2008; Courbasson et al., 2012; Federici & Wisniewski, 2013; Kroger et al., 2010).

**Follow-up:** Abstinence rates and pathology reductions maintained at 3 and 6 months, though somewhat reduced. BMI increases reasonably retained.

**Adolescent ED:** Reduced ED symptoms, depression, general psychopathology; BMI increases for AN patients (Johnston et al., 2015; Safer, Couturier, & Lock, 2007; Salbach et al., 2007; Salbach-Andrae et al., 2008).

## DBT for Substance Use Disorders

Author: Seth R. Axelrod.

### Rationale and Target Population

Co-occurring BPD and SUD: >50% of BPD patients have SUD; >25% of SUD patients have BPD (Trull et al., 2000). DBT-SUD maintains all standard DBT components while adding SUD-specific formulations, strategies, and skills. Mechanism of change: reduction of chronic emotional dysregulation (Axelrod et al., 2011).

### Biosocial Model Extension

Standard biosocial model fully applies. Added emphasis on impulsive biological vulnerability (Crowell, Beauchaine, & Linehan, 2009): negative urgency and low perseverance predict BPD features + alcohol problems; positive urgency, sensation seeking, low premeditation predict ASPD features + alcohol problems. Additional biological vulnerabilities: dopamine system features, differences in inhibitory control/reward neurocircuitry, genetic heritability 30-80%.

**Addict Mind / Clean Mind / Clear Mind:** Under-regulated Addict Mind (governed by cravings) vs. over-regulated Clean Mind (categorically opposed to use, oversimplifies abstinence). Synthesis = Clear Mind: fully accepting vulnerability to use + necessity of avoiding use. Recovery as vigilant, non-judgmental, effortful process.

### DCBA Skills (Addiction-Specific Distress Tolerance)

| Skill | Description |
|---|---|
| Dialectical Abstinence | Synthesis of abstinence approaches (12-step: longer episodes without use) and harm-reduction (relapse prevention: rapid return after lapses). Full commitment to abstinence + complete acceptance that use may occur. Non-judgmental approach to mitigate abstinence violation effect |
| Clear Mind | Grounded awareness of vulnerability + necessity of avoiding use |
| Community Reinforcement | Lifestyle contingencies rewarding abstinence (incompatible work schedule, non-using relationships) |
| Burning Bridges | Remove pathways to use (erase dealer contacts, end use-centred relationships) |
| Building Bridges | Replace drug-associated environmental cues with recovery cues |
| Alternate Rebellion | Non-destructive, values-consistent ways to validate desire to shock/break rules (dye hair, unusual makeup) |
| Adaptive Denial | Deliberate self-statements easing distress of abstinence ("I only have to get through today") |

All DCBA skills applicable to non-substance "addictions" (self-injury, binge eating, shopping, email).

### Target Hierarchy Modifications

SUD placed as top quality-of-life-interfering behaviour (below life-threatening and therapy-interfering). Substance use escalates in hierarchy when: (a) done with suicidal intent or intentional overdose, (b) done to cause bodily harm (drinking with liver disease), (c) medical evidence of imminent death risk -- all become life-threatening. When use limits treatment participation (intoxication, legal probation risk) -- becomes therapy-interfering.

**Blending targets:** Vigilantly address SUD vulnerability factors and controlling variables on chains leading to non-SUD primary targets.

### Path to Clear Mind (Prioritized SUD Sub-Targets)

1. Eliminate active substance use
2. Decrease withdrawal discomfort / use-motivating pain
3. Decrease urges, cravings, temptations
4. Decrease options to use (Burning Bridges)
5. Decrease contact with use cues (including apparently irrelevant behaviours)
6. Increase Community Reinforcement of Clear Mind behaviours

### Attachment Strategies (Addressing Butterfly Attachment)

Butterfly attachment: tendency to flit in and out of treatment without forming bonds, resulting in falling out of therapy. Strategies: orient to butterfly attachment pattern; regular phone check-ins early in treatment; preemptive "getting found" plans (support network commits to helping locate/convince client to return); social networking meetings introducing supports to clinicians; reaching out in natural environments to break avoidance cycles.

### Responding to Lying

Formulated via Active-Passivity / Apparent Competence dialectical dilemma. Replace moralistic responses with phenomenological understanding: clients learned to hide because honesty was punished. Use Level-4 Validation ("years of being punished for not controlling use -- makes sense you learned to hide it"). Level-5 Validation (acknowledge legitimate aversive consequences of honesty -- legal, custody, disability). Level-3 Validation (compassionate mind-reading without accusation). Avoid direct confrontation; minimize conflict by sharing understanding as hypothesis. Monitor lying on diary card as expected secondary target.

### Implementation Challenges

- **Replacement medications:** Opioid agonists (methadone, buprenorphine) encouraged; "overwhelming" evidence for treating opioid addiction. Adjunctive to primary DBT; parsimonious prescribing; limit polypharmacy
- **12-step integration:** DBT and 12-step called "twin daughters of distant mothers" (Linehan). Overlap: acceptance/change dialectic mirrors serenity prayer; skills building; environmental restructuring. Divergence: 12-step = disease model with self-as-flawed; DBT = learned behaviour with self-as-perfect-needing-new-skills
- **Toxicology screening:** Balances DBT preference for subjective self-report with reality of inaccurate reporting
- **Contagion prevention:** Rules against secret relationships, sharing substances; clients avoid appearing intoxicated during groups
- **Case management:** Greater emphasis on environmental intervention (vs. consultation-to-patient) due to frequent housing instability, legal involvement, information release requirements

### Evidence Base

**RCTs for BPD + SUD (6 trials):**

| Study | Design | Key Findings |
|---|---|---|
| Linehan et al. (1999) | DBT-SUD vs. TAU | Reduced substance use; higher retention |
| Linehan et al. (2002) | DBT-SUD vs. manualized SUD (12-step) | Comparable substance reduction; equivalent positive results (prompted "twin daughters" observation). Lower retention for DBT-SUD |
| Linehan et al. (2009) | Multi-site, opioid dependence, men + women | DBT-SUD outperformed comparison for cocaine use and HIV-risk; equivalent for opioid use. Equivalent retention |
| McMain et al. (2004) | DBT vs. non-manualized dual-diagnosis | Comparable to somewhat stronger for DBT. Reduced alcohol but not other substances |
| Harned et al. (2008) | Standard DBT (no SUD modifications) vs. treatment by experts | 87.5% DBT achieved full SUD remission (4+ weeks) vs. 33.3% comparison |
| van den Bosch et al. (2002) | Standard DBT vs. TAU for BPD with/without SUD | Reduced alcohol use but not other substances |

**SUD without BPD:** Courbasson et al. (2012): co-occurring SUD + ED, significantly better retention (control recruitment stopped); reduced ED symptoms and drug use. Nyamathi et al. (2017): brief DBT-CM for incarcerated homeless women, more likely to initiate and maintain abstinence at 6-month follow-up. Beckstead et al. (2015): Native American/Alaska Native adolescents, 96% recovered or improved.

## DBT in Forensic Settings

Authors: Andre Ivanoff, Phillip L. Marotta.

### Rationale

Four bases for forensic DBT: (1) BPD-ASPD comorbidity (ASPD historically cited in up to 50% of incarcerated; shared symptoms: emotional lability, depression, suicide, impulsivity, childhood abuse/neglect); (2) biosocial theory extends to antisocial behaviour; (3) DBT addresses top dynamic criminogenic risk factors; (4) compatibility with Risk-Need-Responsivity (RNR) model.

### Biosocial Theory Extension: Two Subtypes

| Feature | Emotionally Sensitive (Standard BPD Model) | Emotionally Insensitive (Extended Forensic Model) |
|---|---|---|
| Emotional baseline | Elevated negative affectivity | Attenuated physiological arousal |
| Emotional reactivity | Intense, prolonged return to baseline | Hypo-responsivity to stress/anxiety |
| Regulatory pattern | Under-regulation | Over-regulation |
| Behavioural manifestation | Impulsive self-directed harm | Reduced behavioural inhibition, sensation-seeking |
| Environmental transaction | Broad invalidation types | Heavily impacted by coercive caregiving; coercive cycle escalation |

### Risk-Need-Responsivity Alignment

| Dynamic Risk Factor | DBT Skills Addressing It |
|---|---|
| Antisocial personality (impulsive, aggressive, irritable) | Mindfulness, Distress Tolerance, Emotion Regulation |
| Procriminal attitudes | Mindfulness, Emotion Regulation |
| Criminal friends / no prosocial contacts | Interpersonal Effectiveness |
| Substance abuse | Emotion Regulation, SUD skills |
| Poor family relationships | Interpersonal Effectiveness, Mindfulness |
| Poor work/school performance | Interpersonal Effectiveness, Mindfulness |
| No prosocial recreation | Interpersonal Effectiveness |

CBT programmes targeting changeable dynamic risk factors reduce recidivism by up to 55% (Landenberger & Lipsey, 2005).

### Forensic Target Hierarchy Modifications

Standard hierarchy maintained with additions:
- **Life-threatening:** Suicide, NSSI + violence/aggression toward others
- **Therapy-interfering:** + Infracting (preventing group attendance), escape/absconding, operating "stings" (therapy-destroying)
- **Quality-of-life:** Drug use + offence-related behaviours, high-risk sexual behaviours

### Review of 15 Forensic Studies (2017)

Settings: prison (7 studies), forensic secure hospital (6), forensic outpatient (2). Countries: UK (5), US (5), New Zealand (2), Iran (1), Australia (1), Netherlands (1). No published RCTs in forensic settings as of 2017. All quasi-experimental or pre-post designs.

**Treatment delivery:** 11/15 included individual sessions + skills groups; 3 skills-only; 6 reported consultation team. Individual sessions 30-90 min; skills groups 60-180 min weekly; treatment 3-18 months. Training varied from 2-day introductory to 10-day intensive.

### Outcomes Across Forensic Studies

| Domain | Findings |
|---|---|
| Behavioural | Reduced: self-harm/parasuicide, suicidal behaviour, violent behaviour, aggressive outbursts, sexual aggression, disciplinary infractions, criminal recidivism, other risk behaviours |
| Cognitive | Reduced: suicidal ideation, dissociation, BPD symptoms, irrational beliefs, impulsivity. Increased: empathy, positive life perceptions, coping skills |
| Affective | Reduced: anxiety, depression, anger, hostility |

### Key Adaptations for Forensic Settings

- Violence toward others added to life-threatening targets
- Skills examples modified for forensic population language and context
- Self-soothing tools adapted for high-security environments
- Gender-specific examples (many programmes serve women only or men only)
- Poetry, role-play, analogies, games, quizzes to facilitate engagement
- Module order sometimes rearranged (mindfulness first, then distress tolerance)
- Milieu coaching by correctional officers (some wore belt rings of skills cards)

### Implementation Barriers

**Philosophical:** Security always trumps mental health in prisons; "coddling" perceptions from custody staff; contingency management seen as undeserved privilege. **Structural:** Union rules preventing milieu coaching; staff turnover; insufficient dose (1 trainer for 15-20 patients; 45-min weekly groups only; no coaching). **Logistical:** Lengthy clearance processes; facility lockdowns; participant transfer/release; physical constraints (shackles, cages). **Political:** Leadership changes eliminating programmes (documented at multiple US federal and state facilities).

## DBT in Inpatient and Residential Settings

Author: Emily Fox.

### Context

Up to 72% of BPD patients hospitalized at some point (Lieb et al., 2004); 75% inpatient BPD patients are female. NICE (UK) recommends avoiding BPD admissions unless significant risk or Mental Health Act detention. Risks of inpatient treatment: contagion of self-harm, artificial environment limits real-world skill practice.

### Two Programme Types

| Feature | Short-Term (<6 weeks) | Long-Term (>12 weeks) |
|---|---|---|
| Admission type | Acute, crisis-driven, unplanned | Specialist referral, planned |
| Goal | Crisis resolution, discharge with enhanced skills | Comprehensive treatment, discharge with sustained behavioural change |
| Skills approach | Concentrated: fewer skills taught more frequently | Full modules, standard pacing |
| Core feature | Concentrated DBT skills groups training | All 5 DBT functions delivered |
| Evidence examples | Silk et al. (1994): 10-17 days, shortened ER + IE modules; Booth et al. (2012): 8 core skills over 2-week cycles, 4 days/week -- significant self-injury reduction, increased distress tolerance, maintained at 3 months, reduced inpatient days at 1-2 year follow-up | Bohus et al. (2000, 2004): 12-week comprehensive; Fox et al. (2015): minimum 1 year, average 2 years; Kroger et al. (2013, 2014): 12-week with movement/art therapy |

### Five Functions in Inpatient Settings

1. **Enhancing capabilities:** Skills groups (varying frequency: 2-4x/week); drop-in homework sessions; additional mindfulness groups; multiple modules taught simultaneously across different days
2. **Improving motivation:** Weekly individual therapy (1 hour); diary card review, chain/solution analyses; distinguish from unstructured "one-to-one" time with named nurse
3. **Ensuring generalization:** Face-to-face milieu coaching transitioning to phone coaching on leave; "coach of the day"; graded exposure to real-world triggers; peer-to-peer support. Critical: using skills on the unit is not generalizing to real world
4. **Structuring environment:** PRIORITY function for inpatient. Contingencies linking risk-reduction to patient privileges. Transparency in operational policy. Egregious behaviour protocol
5. **Enhancing therapist motivation:** Consultation team integral; address authority/seniority hierarchy issues; equal-status agreement; no blame culture

### Three-Phase Inpatient Treatment Model (Swenson et al., 2007)

**Phase 1 -- Getting In (Pre-treatment):** Orient to DBT (ideally pre-admission); clarify prior DBT experience; build target hierarchy; specify discharge criteria behaviourally ("when you have had no self-harm for 3 months despite utilizing unescorted leave for 6 weeks"); commitment obtained and celebrated. Crucial: establish "life worth living" goals from day one to prevent institutional dependence.

**Phase 2 -- Getting In Control:** Reduce life-threatening behaviours; acquire and practise skills with maximum coaching opportunities; graded exposure from unit to community; diary cards with chain/solution analyses. Avoid "DBT bubble" (unrealistic environment where everyone uses DBT language, coaching always available). Some non-DBT staff responses serve as realistic exposure to post-discharge challenges.

**Phase 3 -- Getting Out:** Focus on staying out of hospital; cope ahead with high-risk discharge situations; vocational/educational/leisure activities set up pre-discharge; test in presence of real-world triggers; scaffold out unit support; robust risk management plan; graduate groups for DBT completers.

### Risk Management: Restrictions-to-Freedoms Continuum

Graduated levels: continuous observation, 15-minute checks, 5-minute checks, hourly checks, bathroom/bedroom access, supervised grounds access, unescorted grounds, escorted community trips, unsupervised community trips, overnight stays. Each level articulated in operational policy with clear behavioural criteria for movement. Contingency management must be dialectical: focus on effectiveness rather than rules.

### Inpatient Dialectical Dilemmas (Adapted from Rathus & Miller, 2000)

- **Pathologizing normative vs. normalizing pathological:** Staff overemphasize unhealthy lifestyle choices (not washing daily, junk food) while minimizing risky behaviour (expecting to get hit, allowing all-day bed rest)
- **Fostering dependence vs. forcing autonomy:** Staff tidy rooms, act as go-betweens vs. telling patients to "use your DBT skills" without conceptualizing which skill or how
- **Excessive leniency vs. authoritarian control:** Removing demands during arousal, allowing swearing/spitting vs. excessive observation preventing self-regulatory skill development. Staff polarization attributed to patient "splitting" -- DBT reframes as behavioural assessment of contingencies

### Predictors of Outcome/Drop-Out

~50% of inpatient patients fail to show clinically significant improvement (Bohus et al., 2004). No psychopathology or sociodemographic variable predicted response. Predictors of drop-out: substance use disorders, younger pre-treatment age, >9 suicide attempts, ASPD comorbidity, >86 weeks prior psychiatric hospitalization. Expulsion reasons: treatment-disturbing behaviour (17%), substance-related (14.8%), violence/threats (4%), suicide attempts (3.4%), failure to gain weight (2.3%), sexual relationship with co-patient (1.7%).

## DBT in College Counselling Centres

Authors: Amanda A. Uliaszek, Carla D. Chugani, Gregory E. Williams.

### Context

94% of CCC directors report steady increase in students with severe psychological problems (Gallagher, 2014). 25% of students report psychiatric treatment/diagnosis in prior year. 52% of CCC students categorized as having severe problems; 8% too severe to remain in school. Anxiety primary concern, followed by depression (37-39% with functionally impairing levels), suicidal ideation (up to 18%).

### Rationale for DBT in CCCs

(1) Transdiagnostic: treats array of presenting problems including most severe (suicidality, self-harm, emotion dysregulation). (2) Modular: can offer skills group only with less training. (3) Group format allows simultaneous treatment of multiple students. (4) Biosocial theory as theory of emotion dysregulation (not just BPD) relevant across student presentations. (5) Flexible intensity to match CCC constraints.

### Evidence Base

| Study | N | Design | Treatment | Results |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pistorello et al. (2012) | 63 | RCT, DBT vs. optimized TAU | 7-12 months comprehensive DBT (modified) | Greater decreases in suicidal behaviour, BPD severity; greater improvement in social adjustment. Drop-out: 35% DBT vs. 47% TAU |
| Engle et al. (2013) | 30-42/semester | Comprehensive DBT vs. TAU (psychodynamic) | At least 1 semester | DBT: zero hospitalizations vs. 13 in TAU; 1 medical leave vs. 13 in TAU |
| Meaney-Tavares & Hasking (2013) | 17 | Pre-post, no control | 8 modified DBT skills group sessions | Significant reductions in depression and BPD (not anxiety); improved adaptive coping |
| Chugani et al. (2013) | 19 | DBT skills group vs. TAU | 11-week DBT skills group (weekly 90 min) | Greater reduction in emotion regulation difficulties; significant increase in adaptive coping |
| Panepinto et al. (2015) | 110 | Pre-post, no control | Modified comprehensive (biweekly individual/consult, 6-13 weeks skills group) | Reduced psychopathological severity and life problems |
| Uliaszek et al. (2016) | 54 | RCT, DBT skills vs. Positive Psychotherapy | 11-12 week skills group | Both improved; DBT: better retention (85% vs. 56%), larger effect sizes for emotion regulation, mindfulness, life satisfaction |
| Rizvi & Steffel (2014) | -- | Two brief skills groups (not CCC) | 8-week ER group and ER/mindfulness group | Both improved emotion regulation, skills use; decreased depression, anxiety, stress |
| Fleming et al. (2015) | -- | DBT skills vs. self-guided handouts for ADHD students | DBT skills group | Greater treatment response for ADHD symptoms and quality of life |

### Strategic Modifications for CCCs

**Treatment structure:**
- Create CCC-specific DBT policies with different parameters (extended session limits, protected consultation team time)
- Dedicated clinician group within CCC focused on severely distressed students
- 68% of CCC-DBT programmes report difficulty meeting/sporadic consultation team attendance as barrier

**Mode modifications:**
- Shorten skills groups from standard 6-month cycle to 6-12 week programme (1 module per semester)
- Skills group 1.5 hours/week (vs. standard 2.5 hours)
- Phone coaching alternatives: business hours only; rotating "on-call" clinician; after-hours crisis hotline; DBT smartphone apps (e.g., DBT Skills Coach)
- Modify 4-miss rule to 3-miss rule for abbreviated treatment

**Intensity options (stepped care):**
1. Drop-in workshops on selected DBT skills (mental wellness)
2. Brief skills group for range of students
3. Brief DBT group as adjunct to individual therapy
4. Standard comprehensive DBT

**Skills delivery options:**
- Distil all skills into single-semester protocol; students repeat for solidification
- Multiple simultaneous groups each focusing on different skill sets (DT + mindfulness; IE + mindfulness)
- Condensation of distress tolerance module; addition of dialectics mini-module

### Implementation Challenges

| Challenge | Potential Solution |
|---|---|
| Session limits conflict with DBT commitment | Create DBT-specific policies within CCC |
| Low administrative support | Use outcome data from pilot programme to justify investment |
| Clinician theoretical orientation misfit | Voluntary participation; hire trained DBT therapist; virtual consultation team for single interested clinician |
| Student commitment/group culture | DBT orientation and commitment strategies at intake; time-limited framing |
| Students knowing each other in group | Direct discussion and pre-emptive problem-solving; role-playing scenarios (campus encounters, social media, shared housing) |
| Dual roles / friendships in group | Dialectic: close friendships cause challenges AND contribute to life worth living |

### Biosocial Theory in CCC Context

No modification needed. Biosocial theory as theory of emotion dysregulation (Linehan, 2015) directly relevant to students presenting with emotional/behavioural difficulties. Perception of invalidation broadly applicable. Dandelion-orchid metaphor explains biological vulnerabilities non-judgmentally: orchid requires specific nurturing to flourish (not defective, different requirements).
