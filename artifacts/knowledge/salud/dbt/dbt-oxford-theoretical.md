# The Oxford Handbook of DBT — Theoretical Underpinnings


## Biosocial Theory: Neurobiological Basis of BPD

Niedtfeld & Bohus (2018). Three core BPD psychopathology domains: affective dysregulation, interpersonal disturbances, identity problems. DBT positions disturbed affective regulation as core feature driving suicidal ideation, maladaptive interpersonal behaviours, and impulsive coping.

### Emotion Regulation Circuitry

Ochsner & Gross (2014) model: emotions modulated via interplay of bottom-up and top-down circuits. Key structures:

| Structure | Role |
|-----------|------|
| Amygdala | Evaluation of emotional valence of external/internal stimuli |
| Insula | Interoceptive signals, negative emotions, pain perception |
| Hypothalamus / brain stem | Autonomic and behavioural responses |
| DLPFC | Voluntary regulation of emotional responses; top of regulatory hierarchy |
| Anterior medio-PFC | Modulates cingulate, which modulates amygdala |
| ACC | Mediates top-down regulation of amygdala |
| vmPFC | Emotion regulation via inhibitory connections |

Regulatory hierarchy: DLPFC and anterior medio-PFC modulate cingulate, which modulates amygdala and subcortical areas (Meyer-Lindenberg & Weinberger, 2006). Regulatory interactions sensitive to genetic variation in candidate genes implicated in personality and affective disorder risk, with gene-environment interactions involving early childhood trauma.

Antecedent-focused strategies: situation selection/modification, reappraisal, attention deployment. Response-focused strategies: physiological, cognitive, behavioural modulation. Experiential avoidance (EA) independently contributes to borderline features beyond emotion regulation difficulties (Schramm et al., 2013).

During adolescence: marked imbalance between increased limbic sensitivity and immature prefrontal areas accounts for high emotional activation and impulsivity.

### Altered Emotion Activation in BPD

Neuroimaging findings converge on limbic hyper-reactivity pattern:

| Finding | Evidence |
|---------|----------|
| Increased amygdala activity to negative stimuli | Multiple fMRI studies; most recent meta-analysis (Schulze, Schmahl, & Niedtfeld, 2016) confirmed; effect most pronounced in medication-free samples |
| Amygdala reactivity to neutral stimuli | Donegan et al., 2003; Koenigsberg et al., 2009; Niedtfeld et al., 2010 |
| Diminished amygdala habituation | Prolonged response to repeated negative pictures (Dudas et al., 2016; Koenigsberg et al., 2014) |
| Enhanced insula reactivity | Parallels amygdala findings during negative stimuli |
| Reduced DLPFC activation | Meta-analysis (Schulze et al., 2016); lower baseline prefrontal glucose metabolism (PET studies) |
| Altered ACC responses | Minzenberg et al., 2007 |

Working memory tasks: emotional distractors produce higher amygdala activity and lower task performance in BPD. Emotional interference impairs response inhibition (flanker task: Holtmann et al., 2013).

Connectivity: reduced prefrontal-limbic connectivity at baseline (New et al., 2007). During emotional challenge: stronger amygdala-ACC coupling (Cullen et al., 2011), increased amygdala-insula coupling during repeated negative stimuli indicating failed habituation (Koenigsberg et al., 2014).

### Altered Emotion Regulation in BPD

Three reappraisal studies found reduced prefrontal recruitment during voluntary emotion regulation:

- Distancing: lower DLPFC and VLPFC activity (Koenigsberg et al., 2009)
- Reappraisal: lower orbitofrontal cortex, higher insula (Schulze et al., 2011)
- Traumatized BPD vs. trauma-exposed HC: both showed alterations, questioning BPD specificity (Lang et al., 2012)

No subjective arousal rating differences despite neural differences -- possible compensatory strategies or impaired emotional self-reflection (New et al., 2013).

Fronto-limbic dysregulation pattern not BPD-specific; present in affective disorders. Childhood maltreatment in healthy subjects produces strikingly similar functional alterations (Dannlowski et al., 2012). Co-occurrence of adverse childhood experiences + dysfunctional emotion regulation + impulsivity + interpersonal problems may be more specific to BPD development (Crowell et al., 2009).

### Post-DBT Neural Changes

| Study | Design | Finding |
|-------|--------|---------|
| Schnell & Herpertz, 2007 | 6 BPD pts, 12-wk residential DBT | Reduced insula and ACC activity to negative pictures |
| Goodman et al., 2014 | Standard DBT, 12 months | Reduced amygdala activation = improved habituation |
| Schmitt et al., 2016 | 12-wk residential DBT vs. TAU | Responders: reduced amygdala, ACC, OFC, DLPFC activation; increased limbic-prefrontal coupling during reappraisal |
| Winter et al., 2016 | Same project | Reduced ACC activity; decreased right inferior parietal lobe during cognitive distraction |
| Niedtfeld et al., 2017 | Same project | Pain-mediated affect regulation (amygdala deactivation via pain) reduced after DBT |
| Paret et al., 2016 | Neurofeedback, 4 sessions | Increased amygdala-vmPFC connectivity = improved emotion regulation |

### NSSI, Pain Processing, and Endogenous Opioid System

Pain sensitivity reduced in BPD. Alteration is affective-emotional, not sensory-discriminative (Schmahl et al., 2004). Painful heat produces increased DLPFC and deactivated amygdala/perigenual ACC -- interpreted as increased top-down regulation via altered pain appraisal. Pain enhances prefrontal-limbic coupling, increasing inhibition of limbic arousal (Niedtfeld et al., 2012). Tissue damage reduces subjective stress, psychophysiological stress reactions, and brain correlates of emotional dysregulation (Reitz et al., 2012, 2015).

Endogenous opioid system (EOS) dysregulation theory of NSSI:

- Low baseline beta-endorphin in CSF/plasma in NSSI patients (Stanley et al., 2010)
- Greater mu-receptor availability in BPD (Prossin et al., 2010)
- Low beta-endorphin associated with negative affect, chronic emptiness, dysphoria
- NSSI triggers beta-endorphin release, temporarily relieving distress
- Two pathways (Bresin & Gordon, 2013): (1) low beta-endorphin + normal dynorphin = dysphoric/dissociative urges for NSSI; (2) low beta-endorphin = upregulated mu-receptors = increased NSSI urges

### Dissociation

During dissociative states: increased prefrontal activity, reduced amygdala and insula activity -- emotional over-modulation (Lanius et al., 2010). Script-driven imagery in BPD: increased inferior frontal gyrus, lower limbic activation (Ludascher et al., 2010; Winter et al., 2015). Dissociation negatively correlates with limbic activity and correlates with insula-default mode network connectivity (Wolf et al., 2011). Dissociation functions as dysfunctional emotion regulation strategy via prefrontal over-activation suppressing limbic response.

### Impulsivity and Serotonergic Dysfunction

Serotonergic dysfunction proposed as BPD endophenotype (Goodman et al., 2010). Alterations in dopamine, serotonin, glutamate, GABA systems (Hoerst et al., 2010). Fenfluramine challenge: HC show increased prefrontal activity (PET); absent in BPD (New et al., 2002; Siever et al., 1999). Aggression linked to tryptophan-hydroxylase 2 haplotype in BPD (Perez-Rodriguez et al., 2010). Frontal-striatal circuits implicated in BPD aggression (Leyton et al., 2001).

Behavioural impulsivity tasks: BPD shows less prefrontal activity during Go/No-Go response inhibition (Vollm et al., 2004). Negative affect mediates behavioural impulsivity in BPD (Sebastian et al., 2013). After negative affect induction: higher error rates, decreased perigenual ACC/OFC, increased insula/dorsal ACC (Silbersweig et al., 2007). Impulsivity in BPD is partly genetic, marked by serotonergic dysfunction, and most pronounced during negative emotional states and stress.

### Interpersonal Disturbances

Heightened limbic activity to negative and neutral facial expressions mirrors emotional challenge findings. Lower cognitive empathy corresponds to heightened mid/posterior insula (Dziobek et al., 2011) -- social-cognitive processing impeded by emotional interference.

Trust and cooperation deficits: insula activation elevated regardless of interaction fairness (King-Casas et al., 2008) -- diminished ability to differentiate fair/unfair interactions. Social exclusion (cyberball): BPD patients felt excluded even during inclusion; elevated dorsal ACC and mPFC indicating increased social threat processing (Domsalla et al., 2014). Pain after social exclusion: higher insula activity; less differentiated amygdala/insula response between inclusion and exclusion in high-rejection-sensitivity BPD patients (Bungert et al., 2015).

Limbic hyper-reactivity impedes functional social interaction by hindering discrimination between prosocial and threatening behaviour.

### Treatment Implications

Across all neuroimaging paradigms: limbic hyper-reactivity (amygdala, insula) + diminished frontal recruitment (ACC, OFC, DLPFC) underpin emotion dysregulation, impulsivity, and interpersonal disturbance in BPD. Skills acquisition moderates successful DBT outcomes (Linehan et al., 2015). DBT skills function as rapidly deployable self-administered mental instructions enabling explicit emotional system regulation.

## Invalidating Environments and BPD Development

Grove & Crowell (2018). The biosocial theory: BPD emerges from complex longitudinal transactions between biologically vulnerable child and environment, emphasizing caregiver-child relationship.

### Biological Vulnerabilities

Three heritable trait vulnerabilities:

| Trait | Heritability | Neural substrate | Mechanism |
|-------|-------------|------------------|-----------|
| Trait impulsivity | ~0.8 | Mesolimbic DA system (nucleus accumbens, ventral striatum) | Hypodopaminergic expression produces aversive/irritable state driving reward-seeking; mesocortical DA matures through adolescence for top-down inhibition |
| Trait anxiety | High | Amygdala, posterior cingulate, septo-hippocampal system (5HT-innervated) | Behavioural inhibition, passive avoidance, fearfulness; excessive inhibitory control = over-controlled/avoidant behaviour |
| Emotional sensitivity | Moderate | Dorsolateral PFC, ventrolateral PFC, dorsomedial PFC, ACC | Propensity toward negative affect; low central DA = irritability; moderates impulsivity/anxiety-to-BPD pathway |

Impulsive youth (especially females) highly susceptible to invalidation. Girls with ADHD at elevated SII risk by adolescence (Hinshaw et al., 2012). Comorbid trait impulsivity + trait anxiety: children internalize criticisms, developing angry/hostile interpersonal style.

### Invalidating Environment Theory: Three Components

1. **Chronic invalidation**: caregivers routinely dismiss/reject child emotional displays; child fails to learn emotion labelling and communication except via intense expressions
2. **Intermittent reinforcement of extremes**: mild-moderate emotional displays punished; extreme/aversive emotional outbursts intermittently reinforced with attention/warmth/acquiescence -- child learns escalation works (parallels Patterson's coercion theory for conduct disorder)
3. **Stabilization of dysregulation**: parent-child dynamics shape chronic emotion dysregulation; emotions experienced more frequently, intensely, longer; evocative effects increase future invalidation across contexts (peers, school)

### Types of Invalidating Environments

- **Child maltreatment**: extreme form; evokes intense emotions + communicates emotions are unjustified; disrupts emotional processing, produces hypervigilance to threat
- **Sociocultural invalidation**: chronic social stress from discrimination/prejudice/stigma based on race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, disability; macro-level (societal norms) and micro-level (racial slurs, micro-aggressions); sexual minority status associated with BPD (Reich & Zanarini, 2008)
- **Subtle family dynamics**: mismatch between child needs and caregiver capacity without maltreatment

### Empirical Evidence

Longitudinal evidence: parental invalidation predicts BPD symptoms in at-risk youth across multiple studies (Dixon-Gordon et al., 2016; Stepp et al., 2012; Johnson et al., 2006). Stepp et al. (2014): 9-year assessment ages 5-8 onward; BPD symptom severity and harsh parenting wax/wane together; reciprocal association confirmed.

Laboratory findings (Crowell et al., 2013, 2017): mother-daughter conflict discussions show self-injuring dyads escalate conflict more; de-escalation occurs only when one member becomes highly dysregulated. Nonlinear dynamic systems analysis: mothers' behaviour drives behaviour and psychophysiological responses of self-injuring adolescents (not reverse). Whalen et al. (2014): positive maternal affective behaviour predicted BPD severity decreases; dyadic negative escalation associated with higher BPD severity.

RSA (respiratory sinus arrhythmia) as emotion regulation biomarker: low resting RSA consistently associated with psychopathology, social impairment, emotional inflexibility. Low RSA found in self-injuring adolescents (Crowell et al., 2006) and adults with BPD (Koenig et al., 2016).

### Risk Factors for Invalidating Contexts

**Child characteristics:**
- Temperament: high negative affectivity, low effortful control, high activity, low sociability, shyness predict BPD symptoms ages 14-19 (Stepp et al., 2014)
- Externalizing disorders: ADHD and ODD symptoms at age 8 predict BPD at age 14; growth rate changes in ADHD (8-10) and ODD (10-14) predict BPD (Stepp et al., 2012)

**Caregiver characteristics:**
- Maternal BPD: more insensitive, intrusive, overprotective, hostile, less engaged parenting; maternal emotion dysregulation as mechanism (Eyden et al., 2016, review of 33 studies)
- Parental substance abuse linked to offspring BPD symptoms (Stepp et al., 2013)
- Harsh punishment: prospectively predicts BPD in adolescent girls (Hallquist et al., 2015)
- Overprotective parenting: Harm Avoidance x Overprotective Parenting predicts BPD (Arens et al., 2011)
- Maternal inconsistency: predicts BPD development/maintenance 2.5 years later (CIC study; Bezirganian et al., 1993)

**Caregiver-child fit**: poor fit produces invalidation even without caregiver pathology or extreme child vulnerability; family life stress prospectively predicts BPD at age 28 (Carlson et al., 2009).

### DBT-A Intervention

DBT-A targets both sides of biology x environment transaction. Multi-family skills group addresses individual vulnerabilities (mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, interpersonal effectiveness) and contextual risks (walking the middle path module: validation, behavioural change strategies, dialectical thinking for parents and adolescents).

## Behavioural Foundations of DBT

Chapman (2018). DBT rooted in psychological behaviourism (Staats, 1996): integrates behavioural approach with cognitive, developmental, biological, personality psychology; emphasizes reinforcement contingencies, learning history, classical conditioning, temperament, biological variables.

### Psychological Behaviourism and Basic Behavioural Repertoires

Person x environment transactions shape basic behavioural repertoires (BBRs) -- characteristic behaviours, emotions, thoughts, actions in particular contexts. BBRs evolve with new learning. BPD: transaction between emotional vulnerability temperament and invalidating environment produces deficits in repertoires for emotion regulation and interpersonal navigation. Withdrawing from rejection = negative reinforcement (avoidance). Escalating emotional expression = intermittent positive reinforcement (attention/support).

DBT is a learning-oriented treatment. Primary purpose: increase behaviours facilitating life worth living, not merely reduce harmful behaviours. Skills deficit model: clients need to learn mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, interpersonal effectiveness.

### Integrative Behavioural Model of NSSI and Suicidal Behaviour

Four-component S-O-R-C framework:

| Component | Elements | Examples |
|-----------|----------|----------|
| Discriminative stimuli | Signals of reinforcement/punishment likelihood | Stressful situations, perceived rejection, presence of NSSI implements |
| Establishing operations | Conditions altering reinforcing value of consequences | Perceived burdensomeness, thwarted belongingness, psychological/physical pain, acquired capacity |
| Person variables | Learning history, temperament, individual characteristics | Prior PPE exposure, personality traits, skill deficits, pain tolerance |
| Behaviours | Actions, thoughts, emotions, sensations | Depression, anxiety, shame, NSSI urges, hopelessness, planning/preparation |
| Consequences | Reinforcement or punishment (positive/negative, social/automatic, immediate/delayed) | Emotional relief (automatic negative reinforcement), social attention, guilt/shame |

Joiner's IPTS: perceived burdensomeness + thwarted belongingness = suicide desire. Acquired capacity via repeated PPE exposure reduces aversiveness of self-inflicted damage (inhibitory learning). Three-step model (Klonsky & May, 2015): when connectedness < pain, suicidal ideation intensifies.

Four-Function Model of NSSI (Nock & Prinstein, 2004): automatic/social x positive/negative reinforcement. Experiential avoidance model (Chapman et al., 2006): automatic negative reinforcement predominant. Matching principle (Hernstein, 1961): NSSI maintained because it provides more immediate, reliable reinforcement with less effort than adaptive alternatives; intermittent reinforcement schedules make NSSI resistant to extinction.

### Chain Analysis and Case Formulation

Chain analysis = functional analysis of discrete problem behaviour episodes. Assesses antecedents, behaviours, consequences. Purpose: generate hypotheses about maintaining variables, identify patterns across episodes, guide intervention. Principles:

- Collaborative, not punitive -- should not function as aversive consequence
- Requires adequate client orientation (rationale, expectations)
- Reinforce client engagement in chain analysis (responsiveness, validation, encouragement)
- Monitor whether chain analysis reinforcement inadvertently reinforces problem behaviour
- 24-hour rule: no between-session contact within 24 hours of NSSI/suicidal behaviour to prevent social reinforcement

Repeated chain analyses coalesce into case formulation identifying key behavioural patterns (BBRs) with specific situations, thoughts, emotions, actions, consequences. Formulation highlights hypotheses and intervention strategies per domain.

### Behavioural Style of DBT

Three behavioural principles shaping DBT session conduct:

1. **Non-judgmental behavioural specificity**: formulate targets as behavioural excesses/deficits; specify topography (frequency, intensity, duration); model specificity; coach clients on observation and description of own behaviour
2. **Active/attentive therapeutic style**: therapist as teacher and coach; teach skills, model use, prompt practice ("drag out behaviour"), observe, coach, provide feedback; iterative cycle
3. **Attention to content, context, and process**: content = what client says/does; process = type of behaviour and its function; context = environmental factors occasioning or reinforcing behaviour within/outside session; therapist monitors whether therapeutic context reinforces problem behaviours vs. adaptive alternatives

## Zen and Mindfulness in DBT

Wolbert (2018). DBT = transaction of behaviour therapy and Zen contemplative practice within dialectical philosophy. Linehan first to incorporate Zen-derived mindfulness into psychotherapy.

### Zen: Core Concepts

Zen (Japanese, from Sanskrit dhyana = meditation). Not text-based religion; practice-oriented. Goal: see reality as it is (Satori/Kensho -- experience of oneness with universe). Three poisons cloud perception: greed, hatred, ignorance. Three schools:

| School | Emphasis |
|--------|----------|
| Rinzai | Kensho (insight into true nature) via zazen + koan study |
| Soto | Gradual cultivation via zazen; deemphasizes koans |
| Sanbo Kyodan | Three treasures (Sangha, Dharma, Buddha); zazen + koans for lay practitioners; trans-confessional |

Koans: teaching stories/questions designed to provoke doubt and resolve it through direct experience of Buddha Nature. Example: "What is the sound of one hand clapping?"

### Linehan's Integration of Zen

1985: Linehan sought acceptance-based complement to behaviour therapy after change-only approach produced treatment dropout and dysregulation in suicidal clients. Trained at Shasta Abbey (Soto Zen; learned radical acceptance through monastery life). Later studied with Willigis Jager Roshi (Benedictine priest and Zen master, trans-confessional approach). Received Roshi transmission from Pat Hawk (Diamond Sangha). Translated Zen into seven DBT mindfulness skills:

- **"What" skills**: Observe, Describe, Participate
- **"How" skills**: Non-judgmentally, One-Mindfully, Effectively
- **Wise Mind**: synthesis of emotion mind and reasonable mind; Zen equivalent = inherent capacity for enlightenment; accessing wisdom of the universe

All mindfulness materials verified for Zen congruence by Jager and Hawk.

### Why Zen (Not Other Mindfulness Approaches)

Zen is practice, not religion -- no religious concepts that would alienate therapy populations. Trans-confessional viewpoint (Jager): suitable for orthodox to atheist. At time of DBT development, only widely available mindfulness-based treatment was MBSR (Kabat-Zinn, 1970s), which stripped spiritual connection. Linehan maintained spiritual dimension within trans-confessional frame.

### Four Noble Truths and Eightfold Path

1. **Suffering exists**: birth, ageing, sickness, death, association with unwanted, separation from wanted, not getting what we want
2. **Cause of suffering**: craving/clinging arising from greed, hatred, ignorance; attachment to permanent independent self; karma = law of cause and effect (behavioural chains)
3. **End of suffering**: extinguishing craving through contemplation and observation, not willpower; letting go of clinging that things should differ
4. **Path out of suffering**: Eightfold Path (Middle Way)

Eightfold Path mapped to DBT:

| Path element | Category | DBT parallel |
|-------------|----------|-------------|
| Right understanding | Wisdom | Understanding causal relationships (chain analysis); letting go of judgments |
| Right intention | Wisdom | Commitment to realization/change |
| Right speech | Conduct | Compassionate communication; interpersonal effectiveness |
| Right action | Conduct | Ethical, compassionate behaviour; "acting as if" |
| Right livelihood | Conduct | Being helpful not harmful |
| Right effort | Mental development | Willingness over willfulness; cultivating wholesome, releasing unwholesome |
| Right mindfulness | Mental development | Awareness of mind and body; observe/describe skills |
| Right concentration | Mental development | Regular formal mindfulness practice |

### Seven Convergences Between Zen and Behaviourism

1. **No independent self**: behaviour therapy focuses on learning experiences and environmental transactions; Zen sees independent self as delusion
2. **Unity**: Zen = oneness of universe; behaviourism = inseparability of behaviour and context; causal analysis requires recognition of unity
3. **Individuality**: both treat each case idiographically; no one-size-fits-all
4. **Non-judgment**: Zen has precepts but no good/bad concepts; behaviourism sees all behaviour as caused -- to change behaviour, change causes
5. **Present focus**: Zen = only this moment exists; behaviour therapy addresses current barriers to goals
6. **Practice emphasis**: Zen requires daily zazen; behaviour therapy assigns rehearsal and homework
7. **Exposure and opposite action**: behaviour therapy blocks avoidance to extinguish fear; Zen faces suffering directly; both use "act as if" (Hawk: "Practice these things as if you already have them"; Aitken: "Act as if you have compassion and you will find that you always did")

### Zen Modifications to DBT Delivery

**Goals**: behaviour therapy goal = change; Zen goal = mindful practice (side effects: decreased suffering, increased happiness, mind control, experiencing reality without delusion). Change requires acceptance first. Radical acceptance = willingness to experience life as it is; freedom from demand itself; leads to joy.

**New therapist strategies**: embrace acceptance alongside change; practice spaciousness of mind; radical acceptance of slow episodic progress; acceptance that working with suicidal individuals involves possibility of client suicide.

**New client targets**: accept one set of problems to work on another; accept past mistakes, present reality, future limitations. Pain exists in present; suffering occurs from dwelling in past or speculating about future. Observe without attaching meaning -- fear vanishes in observation but persists under analysis.

**Therapeutic relationships**: consultation team as Zen Sangha (community fostering practice, instruction, support; team leader as guide maintaining fidelity). Therapist-client relationship mirrors Zen teacher-student relationship: real relationship between equals; guide through difficulties and doubt.
