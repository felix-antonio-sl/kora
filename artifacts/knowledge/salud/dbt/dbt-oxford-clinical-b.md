# The Oxford Handbook of DBT — Clinical Applications (Part B)

## Validation Principles and Practices

Fruzzetti & Ruork. Validation = process: (1) client engages in behaviour, (2) client may/may not express it accurately, (3) therapist attends and attempts understanding from client perspective, (4) therapist communicates that understanding verbally. Core definition: genuine expression of understanding of a person's experience/behaviour and how it "makes sense."

### Targets for Validation

Only valid behaviours are validated, only in the way(s) they are valid. Validating invalid behaviour risks iatrogenesis, relationship disruption, and strengthening dysfunction. Targets include: emotional experiences (especially painful), thinking (non-judgmental and accurate appraisals), wants (even impossible ones), difficulty of tasks, point of view, sense of being out of control, new skillful behaviours (mindfulness, interpersonal skills, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, self-validation, accurate expression, wise mind).

Key principle: validate the valid kernel, not the whole behaviour. Existential validity (person actually feels it) always available as minimum. For judgmental self-statements ("I'm a terrible person"), validate the reality of the thinking pattern, then redirect to descriptive restatement.

### Functions of Validation in DBT

| Function | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Therapeutic alliance | Communicates acceptance/understanding; soothing reduces negative affect, decreases dropout |
| Balance to change | Reduces interpersonal friction; ratio of acceptance-to-change adjusted per client, stage, and context |
| Reinforcer (discrimination training) | Contingent on improved/desirable behaviour; differential validation -- secondary emotions acknowledged, primary emotions normalized with enthusiasm |
| Eliciting stimulus | Signals safety; elicits de-escalation, increased awareness, cognitive flexibility, social connection, skillful behaviour repertoire |
| Model self-validation | Teaches client to identify/describe own emotion non-judgmentally; reduces passivity and external-referencing |

Evidence: Validation reduces negative emotional arousal on self-report and psychophysiological indices even under ongoing stressors (Shenk & Fruzzetti, 2011). Demonstrated in families (Payne & Fruzzetti, 2017), chronic pain (Edlund et al., 2015; Linton et al., 2012). Stronger alliance predicts treatment completion vs. dropout (Wnuk et al., 2013).

### Discrimination Training Protocol

1. Therapist notices client emotion (e.g., anger)
2. Validates existential reality: "You look really angry"
3. Chain analysis reveals situational context
4. Guides attention toward primary emotion: "Do you notice any other emotion?"
5. Client identifies primary emotion (e.g., disappointment)
6. Validates primary emotion at higher level: "Of course, anybody would be disappointed"
7. Differential reinforcement: secondary emotions -- acknowledged/reflected; primary emotions -- normalized, tied to effectiveness

Over time, client learns to identify, label, and express primary emotions accurately. Secondary emotions extinguish as defaults.

### Invalidating Invalid Behaviours

Therapists must sometimes explicitly invalidate invalid behaviour (e.g., inaccurate mind-reading about therapist). Protocol: (1) find valid kernel via brief chain analysis, (2) validate that kernel, (3) clearly state the invalid behaviour is inaccurate, (4) fade direct reassurance over time, (5) redirect client to relationship mindfulness and self-generated answers.

Risk: repeated reassurance reinforces passivity and self-mistrust. Fade therapist-supplied answers; increasingly ask "What do you think?" and validate client's own accurate observations.

### Validation Levels

| Level | Name | Description | When to Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Active Listening | Paying attention with curiosity and openness; tracking nuances of expression, posture, tone | Essential throughout; especially early in treatment |
| 2 | Accurate Reflection | Acknowledging client's thoughts, feelings, behaviours as stated -- no interpretation or addition | Reflecting phenomenology; distinguishing feelings from facts |
| 3 | Articulating Unverbalized | Reading unspoken feelings/wishes/needs from behaviour-in-context combined with history | Client unaware, lacks words, afraid, overwhelmed; offered as hypothesis |
| 4 | Validating via Learning History | Behaviour understandable given past learning or current limitations, even if now problematic | Conditioned responses, skill deficits; often bridges to change target |
| 5 | Normalizing | Behaviour is legitimate, justified, normative -- anyone would respond similarly | Primary emotions; factually dangerous situations; avoid Level 4 when Level 5 fits |
| 6 | Radical Genuineness | Treating the person as valid, capable, equal; not fragile, not incompetent | Overarching stance; includes irreverence, cheerleading, tolerating client's intense emotion |
| 7 | Self-Disclosure | Therapist shares own parallel vulnerabilities to normalize and equalize | Extension of Levels 5-6; must serve client goals; quickly followed by therapist's effective coping |

Cautions:
- Level 3: risk of therapist attachment to single hypothesis; develop multiple hypotheses
- Level 4 when Level 5 fits: invalidating (attributing normative fear to anxiety disorder when situation was truly dangerous)
- Level 7: function must be to communicate understanding, not therapist self-regulation

### When Validation Apparently Fails

Client escalates after therapist's validation attempt. Response protocol:
1. Do NOT insist statement was validating
2. Notice reaction; use Level 6: "I thought I understood but clearly missed something important. Help me understand."
3. If client is dysregulated, coach re-regulation first, then engage in repair
4. Use consultation team for practice, stay non-defensive

Principle: if client does not feel understood, therapist's understanding or communication is lacking.

## Responding to In-Session Clinical Behaviours

Heard. In-session clinical behaviour (ICB) = any client behaviour during treatment session that adversely impacts session or client's daily life. Includes therapy-interfering behaviours (TIBs) and secondary targets. Key behaviour therapy principle: interventions most effective when they stop a clinical behaviour episode as quickly as possible and immediately elicit adaptive behaviour instead.

### Observing ICBs

Novice therapists often miss subtle ICBs while focused on core strategies. Common blind spots:
- Therapists without cognitive training miss overgeneralizing, catastrophizing (notice judgments due to DBT emphasis)
- Therapists without emotion training miss low-level emotional expressions, especially shame
- Unmindfulness during sessions: attachment to "perfect" chain analyses, worry about client reactions

Remedies: review cognitive patterns and emotion elements from DBT manuals; generate client-specific monitoring lists; have consultation team review session recordings; strengthen therapist's own mindfulness practice.

Case pattern (Leala): collaborative, homework-completing client with stalled progress. Team identified dependent behaviours -- repeatedly asking therapist for solutions, generating only solutions involving others' support. Therapist missed these due to client's effective interpersonal skills masking the pattern.

### Describing ICBs

Principles for behavioural description:
1. **Behavioural specificity**: describe form with enough detail for client to recognize exact behaviour. NOT "act less passively" but "automatically responding 'I don't know'" and "automatically rejecting solutions before trying them"
2. **Exclude value judgments**: NOT "you're trying to manipulate me" but "you threatened to harm yourself if I don't extend the session"
3. **Exclude function assumptions**: NOT "attention-seeking" but "using extreme language", "always rating emotions as extremely high", "telling dramatic stories during treatment tasks" -- analyse function separately
4. **Consequences refine but don't substitute**: NOT "pushing my limits" but "asking questions about my private life so often that it pushes my limits"
5. **Shorthand after analysis**: once specific behaviours analysed and understood, labels like "judgmental" or "aggressive" may be used as agreed shorthand

### Deciding Whether to Treat

TIBs: prioritize by severity of consequences, then frequency, ease/speed of treatment, relationship to other targets. Behaviour is TIB only if it actually impedes treatment (knitting in group not TIB if client participates and it aids emotion regulation).

Other ICBs: prioritize by relationship to primary/secondary targets and client goals; frequency in daily life matters more than in-session frequency; select 1-2 behaviours to address at a time; favour those client already motivated to change; consider time required vs. remaining agenda.

### Motivating Clients

Strategies in order of deployment:
1. Relate in-session behaviour to out-of-session consequences and client goals
2. Clarify contingencies (programme continuation, therapist limits)
3. Self-involving self-disclosure: "When you become mute, my motivation to continue coaching plummets"
4. If motivation still insufficient: assess other controlling variables (fear, shame, self-invalidating thoughts) via brief chain analysis of the refusal itself

Case (Martin): refused to address aggressive TIBs. Assessment revealed fear of appearing "weak" and "out of control." Solutions: cognitive restructuring for beliefs, opposite-to-emotion action for fear, commitment to teach assertiveness without aggression.

### Conceptualizing Causal Factors

Use behavioural theory + biosocial theory. Comprehensiveness varies with context:
- Skills class: quick hypothesis without involving client (e.g., each note prompts the next, positive emotion reinforces)
- Phone coaching: focus on emotional links and function (e.g., rejecting solutions prolongs call, prevents loneliness)
- Individual therapy: full behavioural chain analysis when needed

Pattern recognition: content + vocal tone + facial expression + body posture + known patterns from consultation team. Optimal comprehensiveness = degree required to generate solutions that change behaviour permanently.

Common controlling factors: knowledge/skill deficits; aversive emotions (shame, guilt, disgust) prompting avoidance; reinforcing consequences (attention, sympathy, self-validation); classical conditioning to in-session cues.

### Implementing Solutions

**Brief interventions** (skills class, phone coaching, end of session):
- Blocking: physically repositioning, hand-stop gesture
- Silent contingency management: frown, clipped tone, extinction via persistence
- Contingency clarification: "Each diversion means less time for your agenda"
- Cueing existing skills: "Be mindful of your judgmental thoughts"
- Introducing new contingency: "If you continue rejecting solutions, I'll end the call"

**Lengthier solutions**:
- Didactic information (orientation to chain analysis process, diary card scales)
- Skills acquisition: instruction + modelling for specific skill components needed in the moment
- Skills strengthening: shaping, generalizing to session context
- Exposure: break conditioned link between cue and unwarranted emotion (e.g., diary card triggers shame -- repeated mindful exposure reduces shame response)
- Correction-overcorrection: stop TIB + homework relevant to the statement

**Comprehensive solution analysis**: multiple CBT procedures combined. Sequence for complex TIB (case Edie, "I want to quit"):
1. Reduce fear arousal with breathing skills
2. Coach opposite action for fear components, then shame components
3. Examine evidence for self-invalidating assumption
4. Cue mindfulness for judgmental self-invalidation
5. Reinforce work with cheerleading

### Supporting Strategies for ICBs

- **Validation**: searching for validity of client's TIB efficiently reduces therapist anger (opposite action), identifies controlling factors, improves client motivation
- **Dialectical strategies**: enter the paradox ("only when you let go of attachment to control will you have the control you want"); balance acceptance and change solutions; attend to therapist-client transaction system
- **Stylistic strategies**: matter-of-fact tone prevents prolonging TIBs; irreverence disrupts litanies; reciprocal warmth maintains alliance
- **Therapist self-monitoring**: consider how own behaviours prompt/reinforce client ICBs (listening to off-track content reinforces it; asking follow-up questions on tangent prompts more tangent)

## Teaching Mindfulness Skills in DBT

Stanton & Dunkley. Mindfulness = central DBT skill, taught at start of every module, practised at beginning of every skills class. DBT uniquely teaches mindfulness as explicit skill set, distinguishing observe from describe (unlike most mindfulness approaches), and both from participate.

### DBT Mindfulness Skill Architecture

**What Skills** (what to do):
- **Observe**: notice experiences in present moment through senses -- attention to information coming in without words
- **Describe**: put words on experience non-judgmentally, as close to observed experience as possible -- dealing with reality rather than "mind-version"
- **Participate**: fully present without running commentary; move from observing/describing to being in the experience

**How Skills** (how to practise):
- **Non-judgmentally**: identify judgments in content and delivery (tone, facial expression, body posture, thoughts, language); label as judgment then restate factually
- **One-mindfully**: one thing at a time; focus on present, not past/future
- **Effectively**: focus on what works rather than what "should" be; let go of value judgments

**Wise Mind**: synthesis of emotion mind and reason mind. Taught explicitly as skill. Accessed by checking in with both reason and emotion, generating statement that acknowledges truth in both sides, testing for whole-body "fit."

### Teaching in Skills Group

Skill acquisition sequence:
1. Define mindfulness simply; link to client goals with relatable examples
2. Brief practices with specific focus (focused attention type preferred for emotionally dysregulated clients)
3. Feedback after practice to shape mindful awareness
4. Homework setting and review to strengthen and begin generalizing

Practice design principles:
- Keep simple; clear whether focus is observe, describe, or participate
- State how practice starts/stops and what to do when attention wanders
- Eyes open (daily life occurs eyes-open; if client wants closed, encourage gradual shift)
- Short duration (painful thoughts/emotions make extended sitting counterproductive)
- Therapist participates in practice
- Planned to relate to session topic or client-relevant themes

Feedback protocol: questions like "What did you notice?" guide experiential reporting. Therapist labels cognitive events (assumptions, judgments), highlights consequences, sets practice to strengthen/generalize. Key teaching point: opinion is not judgment; judgment identified by whether it looks/sounds judgmental (tone, expression, body).

Non-judgmental stance coaching: (1) label judgment in content and delivery, (2) request factual restatement, (3) coach relaxing forehead and lightening tone, (4) have client evaluate difference, (5) seek commitment to generalize.

### Strengthening in Individual Therapy

Four methods:
1. **Highlighting opportunities**: observe unmindful behaviours -- overwhelmed by memories, future predictions treated as current reality, unexamined assumptions, fighting reality, narrowed attention, passive recipient of mind contents, failing to recognize transience, judgmental descriptions
2. **Behavioural rehearsal with corrective feedback**: re-run specific chain links substituting mindful behaviour. Weak version: "What skill could you have used?" / "Mindfulness" / "OK." Strong version: guide client through exact skill application, introduce problematic thoughts as challenges during rehearsal, give corrective feedback in the moment, test skill under realistic conditions
3. **Modelling mindful and dialectical philosophy**: non-attachment to outcome with relentless problem-solving; each moment unique; therapist notices fluctuating motivation without labelling client as "willing" or "unwilling"
4. **Combining mindfulness with other skills**: mindfulness as "core" skill prerequisite for emotion regulation (naming emotion, checking facts), interpersonal effectiveness (DEAR MAN), distress tolerance

Wise Mind protocol in session:
1. Client states decision from reason mind
2. Therapist guides checking emotion mind: "Sit with it, picture yourself doing it, relax muscles, notice any emotion"
3. Client generates wise mind statement acknowledging both sides: "[reason position] AND at the same time [emotion position]"
4. Client tests for whole-body fit; iterates until statement feels "true"
5. Therapist highlights dialectical, adaptable nature of wise mind

### Generalizing via Phone Coaching

Phone coaching modality: brief, focused, real-time skill application. Protocol:
1. Summarize mini-chain and function of problem behaviour
2. Highlight unmindful descriptions; coach mindful restating ("This is hard for me" vs. "I can't bear it")
3. Client identifies potential skill; therapist models dialectical skill selection ("focus on what works")
4. Behavioural rehearsal in actual environment (e.g., lying in bed, breathing as focus)
5. Therapist role-plays intrusive thoughts; client practises turning mind back to focus
6. Assess whether skill will reduce urges; look for obstacles; get commitment

Therapist self-care: mindfulness practice helps notice and release post-call thoughts about client; supports non-judgmental stance toward client and self.

## DBT with Parents, Couples, and Families to Augment Stage 1

Fruzzetti. Family interventions address four of five comprehensive DBT functions: skill acquisition (relationship skills), skill generalization (practise in actual family context), motivation improvement (modify prompting events and reinforcers on client's chain), and environmental intervention (reduce impediments, increase validation).

### Family Intervention Formats

1. Psychoeducational multi-family groups including patient
2. Psychoeducational multi-family groups excluding patient (parents, partners, caregivers)
3. Skills groups including family members
4. Standard couple/family therapy
5. Combinations

### Family Target Hierarchy (Stage 1)

1. Eliminate physical and sexual aggression/abuse
2. Remove reinforcers for suicidality or self-harm
3. Reduce invalidating behaviours that antecede life-threatening individual behaviours
4. Reduce invalidating behaviours in general
5. Increase family behaviours promoting skillful alternatives (warmth, validation, problem-solving)

### Assessment: Double Chain Analysis

Individual DBT therapist conducts initial chain with patient. Family therapist then constructs parallel "double chain" -- family member's thoughts, emotions, actions woven around patient's chain. Key assessment targets:

**Antecedents**: criticisms, judgments, minimizing pain, blame, unavailability, sexual affairs/lying, gaslighting, forced contact. Case (Maria): mother's scolding ("worthless and lazy") triggered shame escalation leading to cutting.

**Consequences** -- four problematic types:
1. Positive reinforcement of dysfunction (warmth/attention contingent on self-harm)
2. Negative reinforcement of dysfunction (family member becomes less aversive after patient escalates)
3. Failure to reinforce skillful behaviour (improvements go unnoticed)
4. Punishment of skillful behaviour (partner hits client for asserting boundaries)

Also assess: existing helpful family responses to build upon.

### DBT Therapist Strategies with Families

- **Don't make family members look bad**: avoid validating patient in big ways or too quickly; coach family members to validate each other
- **Like all family members**: remember missing perspectives; all behaviour has causes; stay curious and non-judgmental
- **Block and redirect early**: family already practises dysfunction at home; stop dysfunctional patterns immediately; redirect to skillful alternatives
- **Communication strategies**: reciprocal warmth + irreverent playfulness; matter-of-fact tone for problems
- **Revolving door**: send one/more members to waiting room; coach remaining member privately; bring others back for retry

### Establishing Safety

Assess imminent risk at start and end of every family session (diary card item). If same therapist provides individual + family DBT: advantage of knowing patterns and crisis strategies; disadvantage of individual attention potentially reinforcing escalation. If different therapist: advantage of referring to individual therapist; disadvantage of missing crisis chain early. Both require: familiar with crisis treatment plan, good inter-therapist communication, ideally same team.

### Orientation and Commitment

Orient family to: treatment structure, target hierarchy, assessment procedures, duration, contract conditions. Establish blame-free stance. Address common patterns: families blamed by professionals, families blaming patient. Targets: (1) resolve out-of-control behaviours before less-severe ones, (2) build life worth living per core values, (3) create validating social environment.

### Biosocial/Transactional Model for Families

Non-blaming dialectical frame: emotionally vulnerable person transacting with invalidating environment. Key teaching points: (1) sensitive person + ordinary bumps = significant pain, (2) resilient person + repeated criticism = significant pain, (3) sensitive person + criticism = excruciating pain, (4) enough pain makes anyone more sensitive. Both parties doing their best yet still hurting each other.

### Family and Relationship Skills

| Skill | Function |
|---|---|
| Emotion Self-Management | Modulate negative arousal via DT, mindfulness, ER to enable effective family interaction |
| Relationship Reactivation | Spend non-negative time together (exposure to reduce mutual avoidance); enjoyable shared activities |
| Mindfulness | Identify/describe wise-mind goals; awareness of primary emotions |
| Accurate Expression | Descriptive expression of primary emotions, wise-mind goals, desires |
| Relationship Mindfulness | Non-judgmental attention to other person while maintaining awareness of wise-mind relationship goals and love/commitment |
| Validation | Accurate expression of understanding, acceptance, legitimacy of other's experience |
| Radical Acceptance | Release ineffective attempts to change other person; grieve desired changes; notice what was missed |
| Dialectical Problem Solving | Collaborative problem-solving using all skills; awareness of legitimacy of both perspectives |

Evidence: DBT family skills training reduces caregiver burden, depression, grief; increases mastery and empowerment (Ekdahl et al., 2014; Flynn et al., 2017; Hoffman et al., 2005, 2007). Teens of parents receiving DBT family skills reported greater improvement in validating responses, parent availability, and emotion regulation (Payne & Fruzzetti, 2018).

### Eliminating Reinforcers for Dysfunction

Target: move reinforcers, not remove them. Love and care shifted from contingent on dysfunction to contingent on skillful behaviour or non-contingent (fixed schedule). Example (Bill & Cheryl): Bill's nurturing post-suicide-attempt behaviour moved to non-contingent daily warmth + contingent on Cheryl expressing desire for closeness.

Extinction burst management:
1. Orient family to concept using accessible examples (candy aisle tantrum, nicotine withdrawal)
2. Ask patient directly what to expect when reinforcer changes
3. Role-play anticipated scenario
4. Coach patient in distress tolerance
5. Coach family members in replacement responses: express love + redirect to skills + commit to attention when patient is regulated

Case (Rose): daily suicidal thoughts and 6x/month cutting for >1 year. Parents coached to: express love, redirect to calming skills, offer companionship after re-regulation, not discuss suicidality (saved for DBT therapist). Father recognized own distancing pattern was trigger. Within one month: suicidal thoughts and self-harm urges fell to zero; cutting ceased completely.

### Reducing Judgments and Invalidation

Six-step protocol for family members:
1. Identify escalating emotions and judgments about others
2. Tolerate distress triggered by other's behaviour
3. Manage negative emotional arousal
4. Let go of judgments; restate situation descriptively
5. Use relationship mindfulness: remember/experience love and commitment
6. Respond non-judgmentally and descriptively, or not at all

Transactional goal: drive wedge between private initial reactions (emotion, judgmental thought) and verbal/non-verbal responses. Key insight: escalation patterns are longer and more slowly building than perceived -- noticing at lower levels creates more choice and easier tolerance.

### Increasing Validation as Stimulus Control

Validation as antecedent: signals closeness, acceptance, understanding; reduces painful arousal; makes regulated repertoire available. Targets: (1) increase overall validation rate, (2) specifically validate patient's emotions and value as person/family member.

Case (Jennifer): friend cancelled plans. Pre-training mother: "Don't worry, it's no big deal." Post-training mother: "I know you wanted to spend time with her. I'm guessing you are disappointed." Validating primary emotion (disappointment) interrupted escalation to secondary emotion (shame), enabled self-validation, and supported problem-solving.

Notice and reinforce skillful behaviour: communicate about targets so family can reinforce; coach awareness that initial skill exhibitions look rough; address judgmental thoughts about progress ("should do better", "not trying hard enough") using same mindfulness/non-judgment protocol.

### Balancing Change Across Family Members

Perceived unfair burden reduces motivation. Address by: validating concern without reifying "fairness" judgment; assigning all family members skill practices relevant to their deficits; seeking dialectical and quid-pro-quo solutions. Time-out protocol: person leaving states reason ("to keep myself from saying something nasty"), specific plan ("walk to park"), and explicit return time ("back in half an hour") to alleviate abandonment fears.
