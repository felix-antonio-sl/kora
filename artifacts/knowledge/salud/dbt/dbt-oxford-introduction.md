# The Oxford Handbook of DBT — Introduction

## Development history and theoretical roots

DBT originated early 1980s from Linehan's efforts to treat repetitive suicidal behaviour using behaviour therapy. First RCT tested DBT with borderline personality disorder (BPD) patients — a group with high suicide/self-harm rates. Result: first treatment to demonstrate effectiveness for BPD (Linehan, Armstrong, Suarez, Allmon, & Heard, 1991). Remains most researched BPD treatment (Stoffers-Winterling et al., 2012). Other BPD treatments developed since: Schema-Focused Therapy, Mentalization-Based Therapy, Transference-Focused Psychotherapy.

Linehan's intellectual lineage: Freudian psychology → behaviourism, influenced by Mischel (1968) social learning theory, Staats (1975), Bandura (1969). Core appeal of behaviourism: behavioural descriptions of disorders illuminate intervention targets; centrality of data and evidence-gathering (Linehan, 2016). Early career focused on behavioural theories of suicidal behaviour (Linehan, 1981), then grant funding for treatment development.

Three foundational challenges:

- Ensuring client attendance and engagement
- Basing interventions on coherent guiding theory
- Sustaining therapist capacity through treatment course

## The change-acceptance dialectic

### The problem of pure change focus

Rigorous application of behavioural problem-solving to highly suicidal individuals revealed: clients could not tolerate the extreme change focus. Experienced demands for change as fundamentally invalidating → treatment abandonment. Shifting to pure acceptance equally problematic — not helping clients change circumstances also experienced as invalidating. Palliative-only approach untenable for clients in extreme distress with multiple problems.

### Resolution: dual technology

Central dilemma produced two core DBT features:

1. **Change + acceptance** required simultaneously at treatment heart
2. **Philosophical framework** needed to house the contrasting approaches

**Change technology:** Radical behaviourist approach to overt and covert behaviours. Despite manual title "Cognitive-behavioral treatment of BPD" (Linehan, 1993a), treatment emphasizes behaviourism over cognition relative to other CBT treatments. Behavioural/functional analysis as central conceptualization method.

**Acceptance technology:** Linehan sought acceptance not aimed at change. Drawn from long-standing connection to both Eastern (Zen) and Western (contemplative prayer) traditions. Studied with two Zen masters who were Catholic priests. Linehan was first to incorporate mindfulness into a psychological treatment — predating MBCT (Segal, Williams, & Teasdale, 2002) and ACT (Hayes, Strosahl, & Wilson, 1999). Kabat-Zinn brought mindfulness into psychological medicine (1991), but Linehan uniquely translated mindfulness principles into teachable skills for highly distressed, dysregulated clients (Linehan, 1993b; 2015).

### Dual function of acceptance in DBT

| Acceptance type | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Instrumental | Motivate change by validating difficulty | Accepting that changing is hard reduces shame/self-criticism → increased persistence |
| Radical | Complete acceptance with no change agenda | Accepting one did not have a safe childhood; change may follow but is not the goal |

### Dialectics as philosophical container

Dialectical world view: no single perspective owns the whole truth. Reality is complex and oppositional. Tension between opposing views pulls for resolution and synthesis, which evolves into new tensions. Change is the only constant.

Three therapist competency domains driven by DBT's philosophical foundations:

- **Behaviourism** — technology of change
- **Mindfulness** — technology of acceptance
- **Dialectical strategies** — capacity to see both poles, move between acceptance and change, find new solutions

Constant motion between acceptance and change drives therapy speed, movement, flow (Swenson, 2016).

## Biosocial theory and emotion dysregulation

Grant funding required outcome research on a mental disorder diagnosis. Linehan chose BPD given suicide/self-harm frequency. Two problems required resolution:

1. Treatment originally developed for suicidal behaviour, not a disorder — needed mechanism linking both
2. Required BPD model that was treatment-guiding, non-pejorative, compassion-engendering, compatible with research data

**Core construct:** Emotion dysregulation as central BPD problem. BPD criterion behaviours (interpersonal difficulties, identity disturbance, self-harm, impulsive behaviours) are either:

- Natural consequences of emotional dysregulation, or
- Attempts to re-regulate

**Biosocial theory of pathogenesis:** Dialectical/transactional model — biological vulnerabilities transact with pervasively invalidating environments. Over time: environmental responses increase biological sensitivities → heightened sensitivities prompt further invalidation → behavioural patterns of emotional dysregulation in BPD. Theory drives both compassionate understanding and orientation toward solving affective difficulties (Linehan, 1993a).

## Therapist support: consultation team and suicide management

### Therapist burnout and dialectical failure

Treating chronically suicidal individuals produces intense therapist reactions: fear, urges to discharge clients, anger, hostility, attempts to control/reject/attack client. Alternatively, excessive empathy — "leaping into the pool of despair" — effectively abandoning therapy. Remaining fixed at one pole or oscillating between extremes = dialectical failure.

### Two structural solutions

1. **Structured suicide management:** Based on Linehan (1981). Comprehensive problem-solving for recent suicidal behaviours + crisis response protocol (Linehan, 1993a). DBT conceptualizes suicidal/self-harm behaviours as problem-solving attempts where the problem is unbearable and alternatives have failed. Therapists redirect to finding alternative non-suicidal solutions while validating the client's desire to escape.

2. **Consultation Team:** All treating therapists meet to seek assistance on treatment implementation and avoid dialectical failures. Standard practice in psychotherapy RCTs to control adherence — DBT uniquely exported this into the treatment itself as integral component.

## DBT programme structure and treatment functions

### Standard programme components

| Mode | Function | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly skills training groups | Enhance client capabilities | 6-month curriculum taught twice |
| Weekly individual therapy | Enhance client motivation | Target specific problematic behaviours; address emotions, thoughts, contingencies interfering with effective behaviour |
| Out-of-hours telephone consultation | Generalize treatment gains | Skills coaching to avoid crises, increase generalization |
| Consultation team | Enhance therapist capabilities and motivation | Quality of treatment, emotional support for therapists |
| Structuring treatment environment | Facilitate programme delivery | Meetings with senior managers, support groups for families, milieu staff training |

### Functions over forms

Linehan recognized modalities map to functions. Focus on function rather than form provides flexibility for adaptation. Example: inpatient settings — skills groups multiple times/week; generalization via milieu skills coaches rather than individual therapist contact.

Invalidating environments: respond to private communication erratically/extremely/inappropriately, intermittently reinforce intense emotional communication, oversimplify problem-solving challenges → difficulties in labelling/experiencing/managing emotions, tolerating distress, forming realistic goals.

## Staged and targeted clinical approach

DBT addresses clinical complexity through staging and hierarchical targeting:

**Stage 1 — Behavioural stability.** Target hierarchy:

1. Life-threatening behaviours
2. Therapy-interfering behaviours
3. Quality-of-life-interfering behaviours (those that seriously destabilize the client)

Systematic behavioural and solution analyses to reduce targeted behaviours. Over time, patterns emerge enabling more systematic solution implementation (Koerner, 2012).

**Stage 2 — Emotional processing.** Focus on emotionally processing the past once behavioural stability achieved.

### Therapeutic relationship and validation

Therapeutic relationship serves dual function: validating/supportive learning context + arena for resolving interpersonal issues that appear elsewhere in clients' lives.

### Mindfulness as skills

DBT was first psychotherapy to utilize mindfulness as teachable skills. Linehan organized mindfulness skills to facilitate teaching to suicidal, emotionally labile clients.

## Evidence base and mechanisms of action

DBT is the most intensively researched BPD treatment (Stoffers-Winterling et al., 2012).

### Replicability

Examined in multiple RCTs, across multiple sites and countries, by researchers other than treatment developer. All reported similar outcomes to Linehan's studies → treatment learnable and deliverable by others.

### Mediators and moderators

- Neacsiu, Rizvi, & Linehan (2010): analysis of existing study data
- Linehan et al. (2015): dismantling study

Both lines demonstrate: **clients' learning and application of skills**, in context of robust suicidal behaviour management, is central to effective outcomes.

### Outcome breadth

Amalgamated data from several DBT studies (Harned et al., 2008):

| Domain | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Suicidal behaviour | Good |
| Major depression | Good |
| Substance misuse | Good |
| Eating disorders | Good |
| Anxiety disorders | Inferior to standard behavioural anxiety treatments in non-suicidal populations |

Anxiety gap explained by: high-risk individuals struggle to tolerate exposure-based treatment distress without suicidal escalation; therapists avoid exposure due to risk anxiety.

### DBT + exposure protocols for PTSD

- **Harned, Korslund, Foa, & Linehan (2012):** Combined exposure-based protocol with contingency management for suicidal BPD + PTSD patients
- **Bohus:** Developed alternative DBT-integrated PTSD protocol

## Adaptations across populations and settings

| Adaptation type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Minimal (targets/modes only) | Adolescents (Rathus, Miller, Bonavitacola); eating disorders (Bhatnager, Martin-Wagar, Wisniewski); substance use (Axelrod) |
| Biosocial theory modified | Offenders with ASPD (Ivanoff, Marotta) |
| Structural adaptation | Inpatient (Fox); college counselling (Uliasek, Chugani, Williams) |
| Substantial structure + content | Pre-adolescent children + parents (Perepletchikova); intellectual disability (Brown) |
| Skills-only delivery | School settings / STEPS-A (Mazza, Dexter-Mazza); employment-focused PD (Feigenbaum) |

Evidence levels vary: RCTs for adolescent DBT; modest evidence for eating disorders; early-stage for STEPS-A and employment.

Adaptation principle: maintain fidelity first. Modify only after faithful implementation has failed (Fixsen et al., 2005; McHugh, Murray, & Barlow, 2009). All modifications require evaluation given risk of losing effective components.

## Implementation and training challenges

### Implementation

Comprehensive nature challenges routine settings: requires team-based delivery, multiple modalities, dedicated programme structure. Implementation science perspective reveals challenges common to healthcare innovation generally.

### Training

Intensive Training Method developed (DuBose, Botanov, Navaro-Harro, Linehan). Core problem: DBT demand exceeds trained therapist supply; insufficient trainers to train therapists. Potential solutions: empirically-based less-intensive modifications for less severe presentations; online training and supervision methods.

## Key clinical messages

- DBT integrates behavioural theory, Zen practice, and dialectics
- Developed to solve chronic suicidality in BPD
- First treatment to demonstrate clinical efficacy for BPD
- Since manual publication: adopted and adapted across settings, presentations, and diagnoses
