# The Oxford Handbook of DBT — Adaptations (Part A) - Parte 02

## DBT in Inpatient and Residential Settings

Author: Emily Fox.

### Context

Up to 72% of BPD patients hospitalized at some point (Lieb et al., 2004); 75% inpatient BPD patients are female. NICE (UK) recommends avoiding BPD admissions unless significant risk or Mental Health Act detention. Risks of inpatient treatment: contagion of self-harm, artificial environment limits real-world skill practice.

### Two Programme Types

| Feature | Short-Term (<6 weeks) | Long-Term (>12 weeks) |
|---|---|---|
| Admission type | Acute, crisis-driven, unplanned | Specialist referral, planned |
| Goal | Crisis resolution, discharge with enhanced skills | Comprehensive treatment, discharge with sustained behavioural change |
| Skills approach | Concentrated: fewer skills taught more frequently | Full modules, standard pacing |
| Core feature | Concentrated DBT skills groups training | All 5 DBT functions delivered |
| Evidence examples | Silk et al. (1994): 10-17 days, shortened ER + IE modules; Booth et al. (2012): 8 core skills over 2-week cycles, 4 days/week -- significant self-injury reduction, increased distress tolerance, maintained at 3 months, reduced inpatient days at 1-2 year follow-up | Bohus et al. (2000, 2004): 12-week comprehensive; Fox et al. (2015): minimum 1 year, average 2 years; Kroger et al. (2013, 2014): 12-week with movement/art therapy |

### Five Functions in Inpatient Settings

1. **Enhancing capabilities:** Skills groups (varying frequency: 2-4x/week); drop-in homework sessions; additional mindfulness groups; multiple modules taught simultaneously across different days
2. **Improving motivation:** Weekly individual therapy (1 hour); diary card review, chain/solution analyses; distinguish from unstructured "one-to-one" time with named nurse
3. **Ensuring generalization:** Face-to-face milieu coaching transitioning to phone coaching on leave; "coach of the day"; graded exposure to real-world triggers; peer-to-peer support. Critical: using skills on the unit is not generalizing to real world
4. **Structuring environment:** PRIORITY function for inpatient. Contingencies linking risk-reduction to patient privileges. Transparency in operational policy. Egregious behaviour protocol
5. **Enhancing therapist motivation:** Consultation team integral; address authority/seniority hierarchy issues; equal-status agreement; no blame culture

### Three-Phase Inpatient Treatment Model (Swenson et al., 2007)

**Phase 1 -- Getting In (Pre-treatment):** Orient to DBT (ideally pre-admission); clarify prior DBT experience; build target hierarchy; specify discharge criteria behaviourally ("when you have had no self-harm for 3 months despite utilizing unescorted leave for 6 weeks"); commitment obtained and celebrated. Crucial: establish "life worth living" goals from day one to prevent institutional dependence.

**Phase 2 -- Getting In Control:** Reduce life-threatening behaviours; acquire and practise skills with maximum coaching opportunities; graded exposure from unit to community; diary cards with chain/solution analyses. Avoid "DBT bubble" (unrealistic environment where everyone uses DBT language, coaching always available). Some non-DBT staff responses serve as realistic exposure to post-discharge challenges.

**Phase 3 -- Getting Out:** Focus on staying out of hospital; cope ahead with high-risk discharge situations; vocational/educational/leisure activities set up pre-discharge; test in presence of real-world triggers; scaffold out unit support; robust risk management plan; graduate groups for DBT completers.

### Risk Management: Restrictions-to-Freedoms Continuum

Graduated levels: continuous observation, 15-minute checks, 5-minute checks, hourly checks, bathroom/bedroom access, supervised grounds access, unescorted grounds, escorted community trips, unsupervised community trips, overnight stays. Each level articulated in operational policy with clear behavioural criteria for movement. Contingency management must be dialectical: focus on effectiveness rather than rules.

### Inpatient Dialectical Dilemmas (Adapted from Rathus & Miller, 2000)

- **Pathologizing normative vs. normalizing pathological:** Staff overemphasize unhealthy lifestyle choices (not washing daily, junk food) while minimizing risky behaviour (expecting to get hit, allowing all-day bed rest)
- **Fostering dependence vs. forcing autonomy:** Staff tidy rooms, act as go-betweens vs. telling patients to "use your DBT skills" without conceptualizing which skill or how
- **Excessive leniency vs. authoritarian control:** Removing demands during arousal, allowing swearing/spitting vs. excessive observation preventing self-regulatory skill development. Staff polarization attributed to patient "splitting" -- DBT reframes as behavioural assessment of contingencies

### Predictors of Outcome/Drop-Out

~50% of inpatient patients fail to show clinically significant improvement (Bohus et al., 2004). No psychopathology or sociodemographic variable predicted response. Predictors of drop-out: substance use disorders, younger pre-treatment age, >9 suicide attempts, ASPD comorbidity, >86 weeks prior psychiatric hospitalization. Expulsion reasons: treatment-disturbing behaviour (17%), substance-related (14.8%), violence/threats (4%), suicide attempts (3.4%), failure to gain weight (2.3%), sexual relationship with co-patient (1.7%).

## DBT in College Counselling Centres

Authors: Amanda A. Uliaszek, Carla D. Chugani, Gregory E. Williams.

### Context

94% of CCC directors report steady increase in students with severe psychological problems (Gallagher, 2014). 25% of students report psychiatric treatment/diagnosis in prior year. 52% of CCC students categorized as having severe problems; 8% too severe to remain in school. Anxiety primary concern, followed by depression (37-39% with functionally impairing levels), suicidal ideation (up to 18%).

### Rationale for DBT in CCCs

(1) Transdiagnostic: treats array of presenting problems including most severe (suicidality, self-harm, emotion dysregulation). (2) Modular: can offer skills group only with less training. (3) Group format allows simultaneous treatment of multiple students. (4) Biosocial theory as theory of emotion dysregulation (not just BPD) relevant across student presentations. (5) Flexible intensity to match CCC constraints.

### Evidence Base

| Study | N | Design | Treatment | Results |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pistorello et al. (2012) | 63 | RCT, DBT vs. optimized TAU | 7-12 months comprehensive DBT (modified) | Greater decreases in suicidal behaviour, BPD severity; greater improvement in social adjustment. Drop-out: 35% DBT vs. 47% TAU |
| Engle et al. (2013) | 30-42/semester | Comprehensive DBT vs. TAU (psychodynamic) | At least 1 semester | DBT: zero hospitalizations vs. 13 in TAU; 1 medical leave vs. 13 in TAU |
| Meaney-Tavares & Hasking (2013) | 17 | Pre-post, no control | 8 modified DBT skills group sessions | Significant reductions in depression and BPD (not anxiety); improved adaptive coping |
| Chugani et al. (2013) | 19 | DBT skills group vs. TAU | 11-week DBT skills group (weekly 90 min) | Greater reduction in emotion regulation difficulties; significant increase in adaptive coping |
| Panepinto et al. (2015) | 110 | Pre-post, no control | Modified comprehensive (biweekly individual/consult, 6-13 weeks skills group) | Reduced psychopathological severity and life problems |
| Uliaszek et al. (2016) | 54 | RCT, DBT skills vs. Positive Psychotherapy | 11-12 week skills group | Both improved; DBT: better retention (85% vs. 56%), larger effect sizes for emotion regulation, mindfulness, life satisfaction |
| Rizvi & Steffel (2014) | -- | Two brief skills groups (not CCC) | 8-week ER group and ER/mindfulness group | Both improved emotion regulation, skills use; decreased depression, anxiety, stress |
| Fleming et al. (2015) | -- | DBT skills vs. self-guided handouts for ADHD students | DBT skills group | Greater treatment response for ADHD symptoms and quality of life |

### Strategic Modifications for CCCs

**Treatment structure:**
- Create CCC-specific DBT policies with different parameters (extended session limits, protected consultation team time)
- Dedicated clinician group within CCC focused on severely distressed students
- 68% of CCC-DBT programmes report difficulty meeting/sporadic consultation team attendance as barrier

**Mode modifications:**
- Shorten skills groups from standard 6-month cycle to 6-12 week programme (1 module per semester)
- Skills group 1.5 hours/week (vs. standard 2.5 hours)
- Phone coaching alternatives: business hours only; rotating "on-call" clinician; after-hours crisis hotline; DBT smartphone apps (e.g., DBT Skills Coach)
- Modify 4-miss rule to 3-miss rule for abbreviated treatment

**Intensity options (stepped care):**
1. Drop-in workshops on selected DBT skills (mental wellness)
2. Brief skills group for range of students
3. Brief DBT group as adjunct to individual therapy
4. Standard comprehensive DBT

**Skills delivery options:**
- Distil all skills into single-semester protocol; students repeat for solidification
- Multiple simultaneous groups each focusing on different skill sets (DT + mindfulness; IE + mindfulness)
- Condensation of distress tolerance module; addition of dialectics mini-module

### Implementation Challenges

| Challenge | Potential Solution |
|---|---|
| Session limits conflict with DBT commitment | Create DBT-specific policies within CCC |
| Low administrative support | Use outcome data from pilot programme to justify investment |
| Clinician theoretical orientation misfit | Voluntary participation; hire trained DBT therapist; virtual consultation team for single interested clinician |
| Student commitment/group culture | DBT orientation and commitment strategies at intake; time-limited framing |
| Students knowing each other in group | Direct discussion and pre-emptive problem-solving; role-playing scenarios (campus encounters, social media, shared housing) |
| Dual roles / friendships in group | Dialectic: close friendships cause challenges AND contribute to life worth living |

### Biosocial Theory in CCC Context

No modification needed. Biosocial theory as theory of emotion dysregulation (Linehan, 2015) directly relevant to students presenting with emotional/behavioural difficulties. Perception of invalidation broadly applicable. Dandelion-orchid metaphor explains biological vulnerabilities non-judgmentally: orchid requires specific nurturing to flourish (not defective, different requirements).
