# The Oxford Handbook of DBT — Adaptations (Part B)


Chapters 31-36 covering DBT for pre-adolescent children, school-based delivery, employment support, intellectual disability, PTSD integration, and complex PTSD treatment.

## DBT for Pre-Adolescent Children (DBT-C)

**Author:** Francheska Perepletchikova

### Biosocial Model Applied to Children

Biosocial theory extended to child populations: children born with emotional vulnerability (high arousal, high reactivity, slow return to baseline) placed in environments where "good-enough parenting" proves insufficient. Transaction between vulnerable child and overwhelmed environment creates invalidating patterns over time.

Invalidating environment fails to teach: labelling private experiences, trusting experiences as valid, accurate emotional expression, effective pain communication, self-management, emotion regulation. Instead teaches: high negative arousal to failure, unrealistic goals, reliance on external cues, self-invalidation, oscillation between emotional inhibition and extreme responses.

Transactional model (Thomas & Chess, 1985): poorness of fit between child and environment drives reciprocal escalation. Chronic irritability and impulsivity associated with impaired relationships across settings, negative self-concept, increased psychopathology risk in adolescence/adulthood (personality disorders, substance abuse, mood disorders, suicidality).

### DBT-C Treatment Target Hierarchy

DBT-C hierarchy expanded to three main categories with ten subcategories (versus four in standard DBT):

| Priority | Category | Targets |
|----------|----------|---------|
| I | Decrease risk of future psychopathology | 1. Life-threatening behaviours of child |
| | | 2. Therapy-destroying behaviours of child |
| | | 3. Therapy-interfering behaviours of parents/therapists |
| | | 4. Parental emotion regulation |
| | | 5. Effective parenting techniques |
| II | Parent-child relationship | 6. Improve parent-child relationship |
| III | Child's presenting problems | 7. Risky/unsafe/aggressive behaviours |
| | | 8. Quality-of-life-interfering problems |
| | | 9. Skills training |
| | | 10. Therapy-interfering behaviours of child |

Critical departure from standard DBT: parental responses treated as higher priority than child's behaviours throughout treatment. Child's behaviour considered "irrelevant until the environment is able to consistently and effectively promote progress."

### Key Adaptations from Standard DBT

**Behaviour Change Model.** Three factors required for change: (1) awareness of action urge before action occurs, (2) willingness not to follow unjustified action urge, (3) capability to engage in effective behaviour.

**Emotion Change Model.** Emotions have three "food" sources: (1) following action urge, (2) thinking what emotion directs, (3) maintaining body tension. Regulation requires interrupting all three sources simultaneously.

**Terminology.** "Supersenser" replaces "emotionally sensitive" to avoid invalidation and provide dialectical framing of emotional sensitivity as special ability with challenges.

**Skills modifications:**

| Standard DBT | DBT-C Adaptation | Notes |
|-------------|------------------|-------|
| Wise Mind ACCEPTS + IMPROVE + TIP | DISTRACT | Combined into single skill |
| MAN, GIVE, FAST | FRIEND | Simplified; removed "no apologies," "stick to values" |
| ABC skills | LAUGH skills | Let go worries, Apply yourself, Use coping ahead, Goals, Have fun |
| Radical acceptance | Letting it go | Developmentally simplified |

**Delivery format.** Outpatient: family units seen weekly, 90 min (30 min individual child, 20 min parent component, 40 min joint skills training). Active learning prioritized: games, role-plays, experiential exercises, multimedia. "Skills Master" card game for review; "Three-Headed Dragon" for chain/solution analysis.

**Parent training curriculum.** Covers: biosocial theory orientation, didactics on emotions, problem solving, DBT-C skills, creating change-ready and validating environments, reinforcement, punishment, A-VCR model (Attend/Assess, Validate, Coach, Reinforce), dialectics of parenting, walking the middle path.

### Empirical Support

| Trial | Setting | Population | Key Results |
|-------|---------|-----------|-------------|
| RCT (Perepletchikova et al., 2017) | Outpatient | Children 7-12 with DMDD; >50% suicidality/NSSI; comorbid ADHD, anxiety | 90% response rate (DBT-C) vs 45.5% (TAU); TAU had 3x more pharmacotherapy; effects maintained at 3-month follow-up |
| RCT (Perepletchikova, Klee et al., in prep) | Residential | Males with mixed diagnoses; mean IQ 88.9; most had 3+ comorbid disorders | Significant CBCL Internalizing and Externalizing reduction vs TAU (milieu staff report); maintained at follow-up |

Residential trial: significant results reported only by DBT-C-trained milieu staff (not untrained teachers or minimally trained parents), supporting centrality of caregiver training to model.

## DBT Skills in Schools: DBT STEPS-A

**Authors:** James J. Mazza, Elizabeth T. Dexter-Mazza

### Rationale and Design

DBT STEPS-A = DBT Skills Training for Emotional Problem Solving for Adolescents. Universal social-emotional learning (SEL) curriculum developed 2005-2016. Upstream preventive approach targeting NSSI and suicidal thinking/behaviour (STB) through emotion regulation skill acquisition.

Design evolution: initially Tier III (high-risk) focus shifted to Tier I (universal) -- broader reach, classroom-compatible format, proactive prevention.

Key statistics motivating development: suicide = 2nd leading cause of death ages 10-19 (US); 14-39% community adolescents report past-year NSSI; 20-25% high school students warrant mental health services; >80% school-referred cases to outside agencies never receive services vs >95% when services delivered in-school.

### Curriculum Structure

30 structured lessons across four standard DBT modules:

| Component | Duration | Content |
|-----------|----------|---------|
| Mindfulness exercise | 5 min | Different exercise each lesson |
| Homework review | 10 min | Peer-to-peer dyad review |
| New skill teaching | 25-30 min | Skill instruction, examples, practice contexts |
| Summary + homework | 3-5 min | Skill practice assignment |

Recommended module sequence: Orientation + Mindfulness first, then Distress Tolerance, Emotion Regulation, Interpersonal Effectiveness (flexible ordering after mindfulness). Three knowledge tests corresponding to DT, ER, IE modules; mindfulness dispersed across all tests.

Progress monitoring via simplified diary card (skills taught, daily use yes/no, effectiveness 0-7 scale). No personal goals or self-harm monitoring on school version.

### Multitiered Systems of Support (MTSS) Integration

| Tier | Population | Supplemental Strategies |
|------|-----------|----------------------|
| I (Universal) | All students | DBT STEPS-A as classroom course, 15-30 students |
| II (Selected) | Mild-moderate needs | Smaller class (10-15), two curriculum cycles, individual coaching as-needed during school hours |
| III (Indicated) | Intensive needs | All Tier II strategies + designated coaching time (15-45 min), parent groups (monthly/bimonthly evenings), instructor with mental health training, within-school consultation team (weekly/bimonthly) |

### Preliminary Evidence

**Washington State alternative middle school** (n=64-72, ages 13-14): 80-96% reported DBT STEPS-A useful; cross-sectional cutting behaviour dropped from 10-12/year (2011-2014 baseline) to 3 (2015) to 0 (2016).

**Cork, Ireland** (9 schools, n=479, ages 15-16; 385 intervention, 94 control): significant pre-to-post reduction in Emotional Symptom Index high scores (37.8% to 26.6%, p<.001); low self-esteem decreased (32.9% to 25.6%, p<.01); significant improvement on DBT Ways of Coping Checklist.

## DBT Skills for Employment

**Author:** Janet D. Feigenbaum

### Employment and Personality Disorder

Personality disorder (PD) prevalence ~5% UK adult population. Vocational functioning more compromised than social functioning in PD; does not improve in direct association with mental health symptom change -- requires direct targeting. Nearly 50% of individuals with PD unemployed at longitudinal follow-up. PD associated with: more jobs since age 18, less time employed, more "cash in hand" work, more firings, "losing a job on purpose," early retirement.

Mediating factors reducing employment likelihood: impulsivity, neuroticism, disagreeableness, social dysfunction severity, symptom severity.

### Three DBT Employment Adaptations

| Feature | DBT-W (Koons et al., 2006) | DBT-ACES (Comtois et al., 2010) | DBT-SE (Feigenbaum et al., in prep) |
|---------|---------------------------|--------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Format | Full DBT modes | Full DBT modes (2nd year) | Group-only (3 hrs/wk x 16 wks) |
| Duration | 6 months | 12 months (post-stage 1 DBT) | 16 weeks |
| Target pop. | "Significantly disabled" in employment | DBT stage 1 completers | PD diagnosis, low risk, employment-eligible |
| Exclusion | Suicide attempt <6 months | -- | Suicide attempt <3 months; self-harm <1 month |
| N / Retention | n=12, 66.7% completion | n=30, 80% completion | n=41, 69% retention |
| Employment outcome | Small increase in hours worked | OR 3.34 for employment post-treatment; 36% exited mental health services at 1-yr follow-up | 25% obtained employment during intervention; additional 14% interviewed; 5 more employed at 1-month follow-up |
| Other outcomes | Improved mental well-being | Increased QoL, reduced inpatient admissions | Decreased depression, substance misuse, anger; improved IE and social adjustment |

All three deliver on five DBT functions. All positioned as Stage 2 treatments (post high-risk behaviour cessation).

### DBT-SE: Pathway to Working Mind

Six-step pathway: (1) Decrease behaviours preventing employment, (2) Acquire DBT skills, (3) Increase skill use for work readiness, (4) Decrease behaviours interfering with job retention, (5) Increase skill use on job, (6) Working mind.

**Working Mind dialectic:**

- Perfect Employee Mind: "I must never make mistakes" -- anxiety-driven overwork, vigilance
- Unemployed Mind: "I am unable to work" -- hopelessness, inactivity, isolation
- Wise (Effective) Employee Mind: synthesis -- strive for best while accepting imperfection

### DBT-SE Novel Integrating Skills

Workplace-specific composite skills built from standard DBT components:

| Skill | Components |
|-------|-----------|
| FAIL WELL | Find kernel of truth (check facts), Accept fallibility (radical acceptance), Identify opportunity, Learn; done Wisely, Effectively, in the moment, Let it go |
| DEALING with POWER | Dialectical, Effective, Acknowledge authority, Respectful, Interested, Non-judgmental, Genuine; Proceed mindfully, Offer opinions, Activate Willingness, Engage wise mind, Represent self well |
| COFFEE BREAK | Managing self-disclosure and workplace relationships |
| APPRAISAL | Managing workplace evaluation |

Phone coaching limited to working hours (realistic workplace analogue). Chain analysis conducted in group (2 individuals per session, pseudorandom selection, all share at least 2x in 16 weeks).

## DBT for Individuals with Intellectual Disability (DBT-SS)

**Author:** Julie F. Brown

### Biosocial Theory Extended to ID

Individuals with intellectual disability (ID) experience cognitive/executive processing deficits increasing vulnerability and hindering adaptive functioning. Reframe: transactional relationship between vulnerable person and vulnerable environment (not just invalidating environment). Higher rates of: co-occurring mental illness, victimization (30% more likely to experience abuse), social stigmatization. Despite heightened risks, fewer treatment options specifically designed for ID.

Power imbalances pervade relationships: receptive/expressive language problems impact therapy, autonomy constrained by support systems, institutional goals may outweigh individual goals.

### Dialectical Dilemmas in ID Population

| Dilemma | Manifestation in ID |
|---------|-------------------|
| Emotional vulnerability vs self-invalidation | Heightened vulnerability + reinforced escalation patterns + inadequate emotion regulation learning; oversimplifying ease of coping fosters self-invalidation |
| Active passivity vs apparent competency | Support systems oscillate over/under-doing; skills deficits misattributed to passivity; competency dependent on scaffolding level and therapist conceptualization |
| Unrelenting crisis vs inhibited grieving | Recursive challenges (mental illness, victimization, medical problems, stigmatization, congregate living), limited resources; justified overwhelming stress appears as unrelenting crisis |

### Skills System (DBT-SS)

Adapted curriculum for moderate-to-mild ID (Brown, 2016). Nine core skills plus three System Tools:

| Skill | Function | Type |
|-------|----------|------|
| 1. Clear Picture | Mindfulness (notice breath, surroundings, body check, label/rate feelings, notice thoughts/urges) | Base skill |
| 2. On-Track Thinking | Goal-directed thinking, cognitive restructuring (Stop and Check It, Turn It, Cheerleading, Make a Skills Plan) | Base skill |
| 3. On-Track Action | Behavioural activation | Base skill |
| 4. Safety Plan | Crisis management | Any-emotion skill |
| 5. New-Me Activities | Positive activities | Any-emotion skill |
| 6. Problem Solving | Structured problem resolution | Calm-only skill |
| 7. Expressing Myself | Communication | Calm-only skill |
| 8. Getting It Right | Interpersonal effectiveness | Calm-only skill |
| 9. Relationship Care | Relationship maintenance | Calm-only skill |

Design principles: scalable complexity (sub-skills for advanced learners, single/partial skills for greater impairment); concrete language replacing acronyms; reduced cognitive load; visual aids for non-readers.

**Pilot data** (Brown, Brown, & DiBiasio, 2013): n=30, co-occurring ID and mental illness with challenging behaviours. Statistically significant reductions in behavioural outcomes. Only manualized adapted-DBT skills curriculum for this population.

### Cognitive Load Management

**Quick Step Assessment** (Brown, 2016, based on Sweller's Cognitive Load Theory):

1. **Step 1:** Assess cognitive load demands before intervention (complexity, simultaneous processing, emotional content, divergent topics, rapid transitions)
2. **Step 2:** Monitor individual's responses for overload signs (confusion, disorganization, avoidance, resistance, wilfulness)
3. **Step 3:** Adjust intervention (simplify language, use metaphor, break down concepts via task analysis)

Cognitive overload indicators: fragmented sentences, topic shifting without transition, mono-syllabic answers, perseveration, polarity shifting. Therapist must distinguish cognitive overload from wilfulness/resistance -- misattribution destroys therapeutic relationship.

### Treatment Adaptations

| Standard DBT Element | DBT-SS Modification |
|---------------------|-------------------|
| Skills training groups | 60-min groups (cognitive load limit); 12-week cycles (multiple recommended); 1:1 training when group unavailable or dysregulation prevents group learning |
| Diary card | Adapted for non-readers: cue pictures, circling images, check boxes; individualized per client |
| Chain analysis | Uses Skills System framework (Clear Picture of antecedents/problem/consequences; On-Track Thinking to find discriminating stimulus; On-Track Action to evaluate responses) |
| Phone coaching | Standard; additionally train support providers as in-vivo skills coaches |
| Behaviour plans | Pair ABA behavioural strategies with Skills System concepts; encourage client self-advocacy to modify/terminate plans |
| Informed consent | Simple language/images; check boxes; verify comprehension through re-explanation |
| Communication | Continual cognitive load monitoring; simplify language; visual aids; break complex concepts into components |

## Integrating PTSD Treatment into DBT: The DBT PE Protocol

**Authors:** Melanie S. Harned, Sara C. Schmidt

### Rationale

PTSD prevalence among BPD patients in DBT: 36-50% (research settings), 57-83% (practice settings). PTSD least likely disorder to remit during standard DBT alone (12-35% remission). PTSD associated with more frequent self-harm, lower likelihood of eliminating suicide risk, higher dissociation. Self-harm often functionally linked to PTSD symptoms (flashbacks, nightmares, abuse-related thoughts).

### Stage Model

| Stage | Focus | Duration |
|-------|-------|----------|
| Pre-treatment | Orientation to DBT + DBT PE; commitment; psychoeducation about PTSD; establish contingency (2-month NSSI abstinence required before PE) | Variable |
| Stage 1 | Standard DBT without adaptation; reduce behavioural dyscontrol, increase skills; present-focused PTSD coping; exposure-readiness preparation | ~20 weeks average |
| Stage 2 (DBT PE) | Prolonged Exposure integrated into DBT individual therapy; in vivo + imaginal exposure | ~13 sessions (range 6-19) |
| Stage 3 | Standard DBT for remaining targets (self-respect, psychosocial functioning, residual grief, ordinary happiness) | Variable |

### Readiness Criteria for DBT PE Protocol

1. Not at imminent suicide risk
2. No suicidal or non-suicidal self-injury for at least 2 months
3. Ability to control self-harm urges when cued
4. No serious therapy-interfering behaviour
5. PTSD is client's highest-priority goal
6. Ability and willingness to experience intense emotions without escaping

### DBT PE Protocol Structure

**Pre-exposure (Sessions 1-2):** Treatment overview, trauma history interview, select target memories (top 3), commitment to safety/participation, develop Post-Exposure Skills Plan from DBT skills. Session 2: dialectical framework for trauma reactions, in vivo exposure rationale, SUDs scale introduction, construct in vivo hierarchy (includes unjustified-shame situations). Optional support-person session.

**Exposure sessions (Session 3+):** Each session: brief diary card review for higher-order targets, review in vivo/imaginal homework, imaginal exposure (recount trauma in present tense with SUDs monitoring), processing. Additional monitoring unique to DBT PE: pre/post ratings of suicidal urges, self-injury urges, quit-therapy urges, substance urges, dissociation, specific emotions (sadness, anger, shame, guilt, disgust, fear, joy), radical acceptance, feared outcomes. Recordings assigned for daily home listening. Progression: moderate tasks first, hotspots later, typically 2-3 trauma memories for PTSD remission.

**Final session:** Brief imaginal exposure, progress review, relapse prevention (continued self-directed exposure, "exposure lifestyle," high-risk situation planning).

**Session format options:** One 90-min combined session (90 min DBT PE + 30 min DBT) or two separate sessions (60 min DBT + 90 min DBT PE) per week. Routine practice: commonly 90-min single session or two 60-min sessions.

### Evidence Base

| Study | Design | N | Population | Key Results |
|-------|--------|---|-----------|-------------|
| Harned et al., 2012 | Open trial | 13 | Women 18-60, PTSD+BPD+recent self-injury, ~5 additional diagnoses, 11-14 trauma types | 71% PTSD remission post-treatment; 57% maintained at 3-month follow-up |
| Harned et al., 2014 | RCT (DBT+PE vs DBT alone) | 26 | Same population | 80% vs 40% PTSD remission (completers); DBT+PE completers 2.4x less likely to attempt suicide (17% vs 40%), 1.5x less likely to self-injure (67% vs 100%) |
| Meyers et al., 2017 | Open trial, 12-week IOP | 33 | Veterans (51% male), prior incomplete PE/CPT | 67% completion; 91% completers achieved reliable PTSD improvement; large effect sizes for PTSD and dysfunctional coping |

**Safety data:** Exposure task urges to suicide/self-injury: M=0.3-0.6/5; urges rarely increased post-exposure (6-11% of tasks). Adding DBT PE decreased rather than increased self-harm risk.

**Mechanisms:** PTSD remission predicted by between-session habituation in global distress, sadness, anger. PTSD improvement drives subsequent improvement in suicidal ideation, dissociation, depression, global severity, social adjustment.

**Client preference:** 74% preferred DBT+DBT PE over DBT alone at intake; 60% started protocol; 73% of starters completed.

### Implementation Challenges

| Challenge | Solution |
|-----------|---------|
| Lack of foundational exposure training (only 27-28% US psychologists trained) | Pre-training reading/courses on exposure principles; online resources for PE specifically |
| Therapist concerns about exposure safety | Share safety data; "exposure to exposure" via session videos; personal exposure experience; consultation team support |
| PTSD screening gaps (2% diagnosed vs 42% meeting criteria in community MH centres) | Routine intake screening with PTSD Checklist (PCL, 5-10 min, free); establish referral procedures to trained therapists |
| Client ambivalence | Therapist confidence; unified programme messaging; peer role models (completers); psychoeducation |
| Logistical barriers (longer/more frequent sessions) | 60-min DBT PE sessions yield comparable outcomes; plan 10-15 week accommodation period; client-managed audio recording |
| Limited expert supervision post-training | Group consultation via teleconference; advanced case-consultation workshops; train-the-supervisor models |
