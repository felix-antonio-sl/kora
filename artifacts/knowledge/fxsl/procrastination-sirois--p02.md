# Procrastination — Sirois (2022) - Parte 02

## Strategy: Cognitive Reframing

**Affective forecasting biases:**

- Intensity bias: people overestimate how intensely they will feel about future events. Students predicted much worse feelings about exam results than they actually experienced, even when predictions were made <1 hour before results (Buehler & McFarland, 2001)
- Durability bias: people overestimate how long negative emotions will last. Driving test failures rated actual happiness 1 week later much higher than predicted immediately after failing (Ayton et al., 2007)
- Emotional immune system (D. T. Gilbert et al., 1998): automatic coping processes that speed emotional recovery from negative events. Operates below conscious awareness → not factored into affective forecasts → leads to overestimation of both intensity and duration
- Correction: recall similar past challenges and how you actually felt; this activates awareness of coping resources and reduces forecasting biases

**Process vs. outcome focus (Kaftan & Freund, 2020):**

- HIIT exercise program, 4×/week, 8 weeks
- Process focus (enjoying the workout itself) → greater success, better performance, workout felt less difficult and more enjoyable, fewer procrastination intentions, more confidence
- Outcome focus (getting more fit) → workout felt harder, stronger intentions to continue procrastinating if already procrastinated
- Occasional outcome focus can be beneficial: reconnects with larger "why," increases motivation during tough days
- Optimal strategy: primarily process-focused, with periodic outcome reconnection

**Social temptation and declining enjoyment (Sirois & Giguère, 2018):**

- Study 1: students, 2-day tracking of academic tasks; Study 2: community adults, 6-month healthy lifestyle goals
- Declining task enjoyment over time predicts increased procrastination
- Effect amplified by exposure to social temptations (party invitations, unhealthy food offers)
- Hedonic treadmill applies: even enjoyable tasks lose novelty; ~60% of happiness set point = genetics + environment, rest = behavior + thinking (Lyubomirsky et al., 2005)

**Meaning-making as intervention (Yang et al., 2021):**

- Three groups: meaning-making instructions, positive emotions instructions, neutral writing control
- Meaning-making group: goals rated more meaningful, procrastinated on average 2 hours less than control group
- Mechanism: meaning-making instructions → decreased negative emotions about goal → less procrastination
- Effect held regardless of trait procrastination tendency — effective for anyone on the brink of procrastinating

**Intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation:**

| Type | Locus | Drivers | Procrastination risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extrinsic (controlled) | External | Wealth, fame, approval, external rewards | Higher: task felt as obligation |
| Intrinsic (autonomous) | Internal | Mastery, connection, personal development, enjoyment | Lower: task felt as meaningful choice |

- "Have to" → "Get to" reframe: shifts perception from obligation to opportunity, increases felt meaning
- Connect tasks to larger personal values and goals to enable flow states and reduce aversiveness

**Finding the Why exercise — 3 structured meaning prompts:**

1. "Completing this goal will be valuable to how I see myself, because..."
2. "Completing this goal will be valuable to how I connect to others, because..."
3. "Completing this goal will be valuable to my personal growth, because..."

## Strategy: Future Self Connection

Future self-continuity is the degree of psychological closeness between one's present self and future self. Lower continuity → future self feels like a stranger → easier to procrastinate at their expense. Strategies to increase continuity reduce procrastination.

**Time perspective meta-analyses:**

- Baird et al. (2021): 378 studies analyzed — future time perspective linked to better goal setting, monitoring, action, and outcomes (health, academic, career); present time perspective linked to worse self-regulation across all stages
- Sirois (2014a): 14 studies, >4,300 people — procrastination consistently associated with less future thinking (moderately strong effect); link with present thinking was inconsistent and modest. Conclusion: the core problem is lack of future thinking, not excess present thinking

**Self-compassion and Future Self (Sirois, 2016):**

- Self-compassionate people feel closer to their Future Self even as temporal distance increases
- They don't differentiate between temporal selves — "it's all just Self" regardless of time position
- Self-compassionate people are less likely to procrastinate

**Mental imagery intervention (Blouin-Hudon & Pychyl, 2017):**

- Progressive perspective-taking: third-person view (observe Future Self in their environment, notice space, lighting, facial expressions) → first-person view (inhabit Future Self's body, feel breath, look at hands, feel textures, consider exam preparedness)
- Audio exercise, 2×/week for 4 weeks vs. relaxation meditation control
- Results: perspective-taking group felt more connected to Future Self, experienced more empathy for Future Self, and reported less procrastination over the 4-week period

**Letter-writing technique (Chishima & Wilson, 2020):**

- High school students wrote to their 3-year Future Self
- Protocol: (1) create realistic profile of Future Self (occupation, residence, activities, personality), (2) write letter to Future Self, (3) reply back from Future Self's perspective
- Bidirectional exchange (write + reply) significantly more effective than one-way letter
- Effects persist at 1-month follow-up: students still felt closer to Future Self, reported more career planning, better resistance to academic procrastination
- Based on Gestalt "empty chair" therapeutic technique

**Dear Future Self exercise — 5 steps:**

1. Choose which Future Self: identify the temporal "sweet spot" — far enough to create motivational tension, close enough to feel real (typically aligned with a goal's time frame, ≥1 year)
2. Profile Future Self: age, occupation, residence, activities, personality
3. Write letter to Future Self: start a conversation, ask questions, share current situation
4. Reply from Future Self's perspective: fast-forward, inhabit that self, respond to Present Self's letter
5. Reflect: do you feel closer? Has it changed your perspective on your task?

**Key reframes:**

- Future self is mostly present self with minimal changes, not a superhero
- Focus on near Future Selves (tomorrow, next week) to reduce the temporal canyon effect
- Each intermediate Future Self bridges the gap incrementally

## Strategy: Implementation (Ready, Set, Go)

**Ready phase — preparation:**

- Audit the task before starting: identify missing information, resources, or support needed (Lim & Javadpour, 2021: uncertainty about unfamiliar/ambiguous tasks sabotages confidence and promotes procrastination)
- Task doubt vs. self-doubt distinction: missing information about the task (solvable by research) is not the same as doubting personal capabilities (amplified by perfectionism). Important to resolve task doubt before it spirals into self-doubt
- Break complex tasks into meaningful subtasks: each subtask should be a stand-alone accomplishment. Completing each builds self-efficacy (Bandura, 1986: successful experiences grow feelings of competence; each small success increases confidence for the next subtask; failures tear down self-efficacy and breed self-doubt)
- Avoid over-preparation: goal is to reduce uncertainty to manageable levels, not to eliminate all uncertainty (over-preparation becomes another form of procrastination)

**Set phase — procrastinogenic environment management:**

- Procrastinogenic environments: environments containing cues that prime the short-term mood-repair goal over the long-term task goal. Environmental cues (smartphone, social media tabs, guitar, baking supplies) activate priming: bring short-term goal of feeling better to the forefront of attention, pushing long-term task completion to the background (Papies, 2012: food cue research shows environmental cues derail diet intentions; restrained eaters most susceptible; parallel applies to procrastination-prone individuals)
- Remove or quarantine physical and digital distractions: phone in another room on do-not-disturb, close social media/email tabs
- Seed environment with goal-supportive cues: notebook and pen visible, encouraging notes, images related to goals. Salience bias: unexpected or standout cues get more attention → use creative, unexpected positive cues to prime task goals
- Implementation intentions for social distractions (Gollwitzer, 1999): "If [friend calls/texts during blocked work time], then I will [say 'I'd love to chat but can't right now, let's catch up later']" — rehearse aloud until the response comes automatically. Overrides the default of giving in to social temptations

**Go phase — action:**

- Start despite imperfect motivation or mood: motivation emerges from action, not before it. Taking action generates positive emotions, builds self-efficacy, and creates the motivation assumed to be a prerequisite
- No "perfect time" exists: belief in perfect timing is a hindsight bias artifact — selective recall of past successes creates the illusion that conditions were perfectly aligned. Perfect timing is a retrospective concept, not a predictive one
- Small initial successes build momentum: completing the first subtask provides evidence of capability, which motivates persistence through subsequent subtasks
- William James: "Belief creates the actual fact"
- Lao Tzu: "The journey of a thousand miles begins with one step"

**Common myths debunked:**

| Myth | Reality | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Wait until motivated to start | Motivation follows action, not vice versa | Behavioral observation; self-efficacy theory (Bandura, 1986) |
| Work better under last-minute pressure | Last-minute work produces inferior results | Kim & Seo, 2015 (meta-analysis) |
| Perfect timing exists | Hindsight bias creates illusion of optimal moment | Cognitive bias research |
| Future self will be more capable | Future self ≈ present self with minimal change | Ersner-Hershfield et al., 2009 |

## Measurement Tools

**GPS-9 — General Procrastination Scale, 9 items (Sirois, Yang, & van Eerde, 2019):**

- 9 statements rated 1 (false) to 5 (true for me)
- Items 5, 7, 9 are reverse-scored (1↔5, 2↔4)
- Score = sum of all 9 items ÷ 9 (average out of 5)
- 1.0–2.0: low; 2.1–3.5: moderate; 3.6–5.0: high trait procrastination

**Future Self Continuity Scale (Ersner-Hershfield et al., 2009):**

- Overlapping circles rated 1 (very distant, like a stranger) to 7 (very close, like a friend)
