# Principle-based Organizational Structure


## Summary

Organizational structure is an engineering science with firm principles—not a matter of personalities, politics, or personal style. This document presents seven principles for designing high-performance structures, five building blocks common to all organizations, a diagnostic method (Rainbow Analysis), and guidance for teamwork processes and special situations. Well-designed, principle-based structure is a lasting investment that evolves naturally without repeated restructuring.

## Core Thesis

**Organizational Machine:** Most managers focus on operating a poorly designed machine rather than redesigning it (Sergio Paiz). Leaders must pursue three parallel strategic vectors simultaneously: Market (alignment with customers), Technical (product/service capabilities), and Organizational (the machine itself—addressed here).

**Structure Primacy:** Of all key elements of organizational design (structure, culture, resource-governance processes, methods, metrics), structure is the most powerful.

**Structure Definition:** The organization chart defining everybody's domains, combined with the processes that assemble those specialists on teams.

## Benefits of Healthy Structure and Symptoms of Poor Structure

### Benefits of Healthy Structure

- Individual accountabilities clear; people deliver results.
- Staff focused on excellence in single profession; perform better.
- Staff customer-focused; client relationships improve; needs/strategies well-aligned.
- Teams form spontaneously; work well together in flexible, well-defined processes.
- Organization delivers commitments reliably, quickly, with lower risk.
- Costs competitive; redundancies eliminated; internal entrepreneurs accountable for best value.
- Product quality increases; product line better integrated.
- Staff creative and entrepreneurial; innovation pace improves in every discipline.
- Great place to work; staff empowered; conflicts of interests eliminated.
- Structure lasting; not designed around incumbent personalities; automatically evolves with strategies and technologies.
- Well-designed structure contributes directly to organizational mission/shareholder value.

### Symptoms of Poor Structure

- Unclear individual accountabilities; confusion about who does what; redundant efforts; territorial disputes; internal competition.
- Poor performance; jobs too big for most people to succeed; need for all "A players."
- Lack of customer focus; weak client relationships; initiatives product/technology (not business) driven.
- Difficulties with cross-boundary teamwork; slow response; independent "silos."
- Slow or unreliable delivery; high costs; empire building.
- Poor quality; poorly integrated product line; lack of entrepreneurial spirit.
- Low morale; disempowerment; dead-end jobs; cynicism; stress.
- Repeated restructurings, each fixing some problems while creating others.

**Consequence:** Unhealthy structure is neither "supplier of choice" to customers nor "employer of choice" to staff; stressful place to work; leaves little time for strategic thinking.

## Science of Structure

Organizational structure is engineering science based on real-world empirical observations, generalized to principles and frameworks, tested over decades. Principles are now clear enough that examining any organization chart reveals who is fighting with whom, who is not meeting objectives, who has ulcers.

### Why Study the Science

| Reason | Benefit |
|--------|---------|
| Avoid "Organization du Jour" | Principle-based structure makes deliberate trade-off decisions; performs well across challenges; dynamically adapts; lasts |
| Easier to explain | With clear principles, can invite leadership participation without endless haggling; participation builds commitment |
| Don't blame people | Critical to distinguish poorly performing person from good person in poorly designed job; futile and cruel to blame people for structurally caused problems |
| New performance levels | Consistently leads to improved role clarity, better teamwork, customer focus, entrepreneurship, improved performance |

### Why Great People and Great Processes Alone Are Insufficient

**Great People insufficiency:**
- Constant restructuring required whenever key people change jobs.
- Organizations dependent on great people are fragile; generally fall apart when charismatic leader or key members leave.
- Requiring everyone to be above average is impractical (can't always afford best; limited growth potential).
- Key insight: Great people in bad structure will fail, or at least not perform to potential. Normal bell-curve of people in healthy structure can succeed.

**Great Processes insufficiency:**
- If no group specializes in a discipline, processes depending on that competency fail.
- Structure can override processes: when staff caught between structure and process, they optimize performance appraisals.
- Processes only make sense for highly routine work; in creative/flexible functions, rigidly defined processes increase rigidity and reduce creativity.

**Structure Drives Performance:** Structure is the foundation on which good people and good processes thrive. It is one of the most powerful drivers of efficiency, effectiveness, agility, quality, creativity, innovation, competitiveness, and customer satisfaction—and a key element of staff competence, job satisfaction, motivation, and happiness.

## Seven Fundamental Principles

| Principle | Statement |
|-----------|-----------|
| Principle 1 | Golden Rule: Authority and accountability must match. |
| Principle 2 | Specialization and Teamwork: You can only be world-class at one thing at a time; but you can't specialize if you can't team. |
| Principle 3 | Precise Domains: Define clear boundaries with no overlaps or gaps. |
| Principle 4 | Basis for Substructure: Divide function into groups based on what it's supposed to be good at. |
| Principle 5 | Avoid Conflicts of Interests: Don't expect people to go in two opposing directions. |
| Principle 6 | Cluster by Professional Synergies: Cluster groups under common boss based on similar professions. |
| Principle 7 | Business Within a Business: Every manager is entrepreneur whose job is to satisfy customers with products and services. |

## Principle 1: Golden Rule — Authority and Accountability Must Match

**Most important principle; absolutely essential.**

**Consequences of violation:**

| Violation | Result |
|-----------|--------|
| Authority without accountability | Person becomes unconstrained tyrant; makes decisions without bearing consequences; issues edicts, lets others take blame |
| Accountability without authority | People are disempowered; cannot be held accountable for results without resources and authorities to achieve them; leads to helpless "victim" mentality over time |

**Empowerment:**
- Everybody accountable for own results, with authority over all information, resources, decisions needed.
- Not anarchy; people must have no more authorities than warranted by accountabilities. Fixed-sum equation: if anyone has more authorities than accountabilities, somebody else has less.
- Empowerment not optional; absolute necessity in competitive environment; fundamental to employee motivation.

**Empowerment at every level:** Even relatively junior people can be empowered with matched authorities/accountabilities. For inexperienced subordinates: empower in smaller chunks (series of small assignments within capabilities), rather than disempowering by dictating how to do jobs.

**Mandating "How" vs. "What":** Organizations should hold people accountable for results, leaving them free (within bounds) to decide how. Defining deliverables ("what") is not disempowering. Requiring specific "how" is disempowering (e.g., mandating specific documentation format, specific project-management method regardless of project size).

**Leader's specific authorities and accountabilities in an empowered organization:**
- Decide rules of game (structure, resource-governance processes, culture).
- Adjust domains; arbitrate boundary disputes; ensure teamwork.
- Develop talent (recruiting, inspiring, coaching—coaching as advice, not mandate).
- Manage performance (objectives, feedback, measuring results, rewards, performance problems).
- Manage commitments and resources (assign work within group).
- Coordinate shared decisions (common methods, professional practices).
- Make decisions where consensus cannot be reached.
- Guide business and product strategies within group.
- Serve as diplomat representing group to peers and superiors.

**Leader's job summary:** Create the right environment; do everything possible to help subordinates succeed.

**Managing by results gives more, not less, control:** Focus moves from micro-managing to orchestrating right results; leader becomes driver of machine, not cog.

**Implications for structure:**
- Define jobs by what people produce, not by how they do work. Job descriptions phrased as "accountable for delivering," not "responsible for doing."
- Never create a job whose purpose is to disempower others.
- Never create steering committees or process owners ("integrating managers") who constrain others' prerogatives.
- To address teamwork concerns: install systemic processes that align priorities and link teams across boundaries without disempowerment.

## Principle 2: Specialization and Teamwork

**Why organizations exist:** Organizations allow people to specialize. When T-shaped specialists in diverse fields work together, the organization has both depth and breadth, processing more variety than any individual.

**VARIETY = COMPLEXITY × PACE** (cybernetics): Measure of throughput/bandwidth; amount and diversity of information confronting organization at any point in time.

**Three choices for finite brain cycles:**

| Choice | Description | Result |
|--------|-------------|--------|
| Generalist | Know a little about everything | Many things, none particularly well; never master any field |
| Extreme Specialist | Focus all on one topic, know nothing else | Excel at one thing; can't work with others; impractical |
| T-shaped Specialist | Focus on one profession + sufficient breadth for effective collaboration | Definition of practical specialist; world-class expertise + teamwork capability |

**Benefits of specialization:**
- **Productivity:** More efficient; translates to cost savings.
- **Speed:** Ready answers, no learning curve per challenge; latest methods/technologies.
- **Quality:** Specialists know how; products more usable, capable, maintainable, lower life-cycle costs.
- **Risk:** Competence and experience deliver results more reliably, fewer risks.
- **Innovation:** Stay current with literature; first to learn emerging technologies.
- **Reduced stress:** Confident in abilities; more stable, productive, happy.
- **Motivation:** Greater competence → easier to excel; better career opportunities.

Specialists always outperform generalists. Adam Smith described advantages of division of labor in 1776.

**Specialization requires teamwork:**
- More people specialize, more interdependent they become.
- Organization of specialists without teamwork performs worse than collection of independent generalists.
- If organization not good at cross-boundary teamwork, must back away from specialization → "silo" organization.

**The Silo Organization:** Created when leaders put all skills under one manager to minimize interdependencies. Result: each profession scattered among various groups → fragmentation → lower performance. Antidote to silos is effective cross-boundary teamwork.

**Implications for structure:**
- Design jobs around well-focused specialties, not generalists.
- Invest in cross-boundary teamwork mechanisms; more teamwork capability = more possible specialization = better performance.
- Organizations exist to allow specialization, which depends on effective teamwork.

## Principle 3: Precise Domains

**Domain definition:** Each group's boundaries. Vague domains cause three problems: overlaps, gaps, lack of focus.

**Costs of overlapping domains:**
- Reduced specialization (lower productivity, delivery, quality; greater risk; less innovation; more stress).
- Redundant efforts (parallel research and production; reinvention instead of reuse).
- Less innovation (resources duplicated elsewhere preclude exploring other fields).
- Confusion (customers don't know where to go for what).
- Product dis-integration (multiple incompatible versions of same product).
- Less teamwork (inevitable friction from internal competition undermines teamwork; no team-building can overcome structural friction).
- Lack of entrepreneurship (no one feels ownership as entrepreneur accountable for full performance of line of business).

Internal competition should never be created—whether deliberately or through vague boundaries.

**Consequences of gaps:**
- Unreliable delivery: functions done only when urgent or when spare time allows.
- Reduced specialization: staff filling gaps outside specialty aren't experts.
- Overlaps: groups that need missing specialty fill gap themselves, creating overlaps over time.

**Focus benefits of clear domains:**
- Tell people what to study, what conferences to attend, what methods/technologies to master.
- Staff develop pride in group's unique function.
- Basis for effective performance management (objectives can only be clearly defined if jobs clearly defined).
- Customers and staff know where to go for help.

**Domains based on results, not roles or tasks:**
- Key to good domains: bound what people produce, not what they do.
- Dividing up results is far easier than sorting tasks.
- By defining who produces what, you automatically know who does what.
- Clear domains define deliverables, empowering groups to do what it takes to deliver them.

**Problems with "roles and responsibilities" (RACI):**
- Define accountabilities for tasks and processes, not results.
- RACI Accountable person (with authority to approve) may not be the customer; risks separating authority from accountability.
- Fixed Consulted/Informed lists may not be right stakeholders for every project; bureaucratic, not flexible.

**Implications for structure:**
- Provide every group with precisely worded boundaries (not just "few words in a box"—each box needs carefully worded paragraph).
- No overlaps; no internal competition.
- No gaps; every needed specialty within domain of one group.
- Domains bound what people produce.

## Principle 4: Basis for Substructure

**Definition:** The basis for substructure is the way domains are divided among groups within a profession. It determines people's specialties (their "bottom of the T").

**Examples of different bases:**
- By clients' business processes → experts on workflows, generalists on clients/engineering/services.
- By clients (business units / territories) → close to clients, generalists on products/services.
- By technologies/disciplines → experts on specific products, generalists on clients.
- By services → experts on service delivery, generalists on clients and products.

**Consequences of wrong basis for substructure:**
- Reduced specialization → lower productivity, slower delivery, lower quality, more risk, less innovation, more stress.
- Domain overlaps → confusion, redundant efforts, product dis-integration, territorial friction, lack of entrepreneurship.
- Inappropriate biases → poor advice; groups optimize wrong objectives.
- Disempowerment → lack of customer focus and entrepreneurship, less business planning.

**Summary of inappropriate substructure consequences:**

| Problem | Impact |
|---------|--------|
| Reduced specialization [Principle 2] | Lower productivity, slower delivery, lower quality, greater risk, less innovation, more stress, lower motivation |
| Domain overlaps [Principle 3] | Redundant efforts, less innovation, confusion, product dis-integration, territorial friction, lack of entrepreneurship |
| Inappropriate biases [Principle 4] | Poor advice, optimizing wrong objectives |
| Disempowerment [Principles 1, 7] | Lack of customer focus, lack of entrepreneurship, less business planning |

## Principle 5: Avoid Conflicts of Interests

Don't expect people to go in two opposing directions. Structure should not create jobs where the person's accountabilities pull in fundamentally incompatible directions (e.g., assigning both innovation and operational stability to the same person/group, or assigning both technical specialist and unbiased client diagnosis to the same person).
