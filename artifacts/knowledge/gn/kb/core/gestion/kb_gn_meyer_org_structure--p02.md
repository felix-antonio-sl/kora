# Principle-based Organizational Structure - Parte 02

## Principle 6: Cluster by Professional Synergies

Cluster groups under a common boss based on similar professions. Shared professional context enables productive exchange, common standards, shared methods, and mutual coaching. Non-synergistic clustering (e.g., grouping unlike disciplines under one executive) reduces professional collaboration and creates managerial challenge of leading diverse fields simultaneously.

## Principle 7: Business Within a Business

Every manager is an entrepreneur accountable for satisfying customers (internal and external) with products and services. Groups are lines of business. Empowered entrepreneurs:
- Plan future of their businesses; develop strategies, invest in capabilities, evolve product lines.
- Focus on customers: funded (given budget) to serve customers, not to maintain bureaucratic territories.
- Optimize costs and methods continually.
- Make commitments they can keep; keep every commitment.

## Building Blocks of Organization Charts

Building Blocks are the lines of business that exist within all organizations (corporations, not-for-profits, higher education, governments). The same Building Blocks appear within departments (IT, HR, Finance, Facilities). Five types at high level:

### Building Block 1: Engineers

**Definition:** Create organization's products; discipline or technology specialists, designers, gurus in specific products.

**Services (all based on in-depth product-design expertise):**
- Solutions (entirely new or enhancements)
- Repairs
- Configuration tuning
- Domain-specific incident management (second-level support)
- Documentation, training materials, training
- Expert time, studies, presentations, sales support

**Applications vs. Base Engineers:**

| Type | Role | Examples |
|------|------|---------|
| Applications Engineers | Assemble components into purpose-specific products (top layer) | Cars and trucks designers; Boeing airplane designers; university schools |
| Base Engineers | Design purpose-independent components used by various applications (lower layers) | Engine/transmission designers; IT infrastructure (compute, database, middleware, networks, end-user tools) |

**Competencies profile:**

| Skill | Level |
|-------|-------|
| Technical skill | High |
| Project management | High |
| Service management (operations) | Low |
| Business of customers | Low |
| Interpersonal skills | Low |
| People supervision | Medium |

**Biases (Five Fundamental Conflicts):**

| Conflict | Engineers' Bias |
|----------|----------------|
| Invention vs. operational stability | Invention |
| Purpose-specific vs. components | Applications (purpose-specific); Base (components) |
| Enterprisewide vs. specialist | Specialist |
| Technical specialization vs. unbiased diagnosis of clients' needs | Technical specialization |
| Service oriented vs. audit | Service oriented |

### Building Block 2: Service Providers

**Definition:** Deliver ongoing services; either buy products from Engineers and use those assets to deliver service, or deliver services primarily through people.

**Asset-based Service Providers:** Services based on ownership of shared-use assets (infrastructure). Examples: computer time/storage/connectivity; hospital rooms/clinics/labs; roads/ports/parks (government).

**People-based Service Providers:** Services produced by people; three types:
- **Product support:** Help customers get value from organization's products (e.g., customer service, training).
- **Internal support:** Leverage time and enhance abilities of others (e.g., PMO providing project planning services—not project management; field technicians; technical writers).
- **Business services:** Services outside organization's primary intent (Administration, IT, Finance, HR departments from CEO's perspective).

**Competencies profile:**

| Skill | Asset-based | People-based |
|-------|-------------|--------------|
| Technical skill | Medium | Medium |
| Project management | Low | Low |
| Service management | High | High |
| Business of customers | Low | Low |
| Interpersonal skills | Low | High |
| People supervision | Medium | High |

**Biases:**

| Conflict | Service Providers' Bias |
|----------|------------------------|
| Invention vs. operational stability | Operational stability |
| Enterprisewide vs. specialist | Specialist |
| Technical specialization vs. unbiased client diagnosis | Unbiased |
| Service oriented vs. audit | Service oriented |

### Building Block 3: Coordinators

**Definition:** Drive shared decisions—policies, plans, standards—that require consensus among stakeholders.

**Activities:**
- Establish decision-making processes; enable with methods and project plans.
- Ensure right stakeholders involved; provide common information (assumptions, templates, frameworks, timeframes).
- Facilitate collaboration on interdependencies.
- Compile results and make available; help others find, interpret, and apply shared decisions.

**Critical distinctions:**
- Coordinators are only accountable for effective consensus-based decision-making processes, not for content.
- If stakeholders cannot reach consensus, Coordinators cannot force decisions (doing so would violate Principle 1).
- Coordinators are not a participant in decisions; they facilitate the process.
- Compliance, quality assurance, security, business continuity, planning—all can be Coordinator functions when structured correctly.

**Compliance as Coordinator (vs. Scapegoat Trap):**
- Wrong approach: Chief Compliance Officer given authority over others' compliance → they own accountability for others' behaviors → scapegoat trap.
- Right approach: Hold everyone accountable for own compliance; Compliance Officer serves as subject-matter expert, educator, process facilitator—not enforcer.
- If oversight (Audit) needed, must be kept arm's-length from service-oriented Coordinators.

### Building Block 4: Sales and Marketing (Account Sales / Retail Sales)

**Account Sales (for internal service providers):** Dedicated relationship managers who focus on understanding specific clients' business strategies and needs; diagnose clients' challenges and opportunities; bring full range of organization's offerings to bear on those needs. Accountable for relationships and strategic alignment—not for delivering services.

**Account Sales biases:** Customer/business driven; not biased toward any particular product or technology; focused on what best serves the client.

**Retail Sales (for consumer organizations):** Sell to market broadly; focus on customer types rather than individual accounts.

### Building Block 5: Audit

**Definition:** Independent assurance function; examines whether organization is complying with own policies, standards, and external requirements; reports directly to an appropriate authority (not to those being audited).

**Critical structural requirement:** Must be organizationally independent of those whose behaviors it audits. Cannot simultaneously serve as service-oriented Coordinator and auditor.

## Rainbow Analysis: Structural Diagnostics

Rainbow Analysis is the primary diagnostic method for identifying structural problems in an organization chart. Four structural problems to detect:

### Problem 1: Gaps

A needed line of business exists nowhere in the organization. Consequences: unreliable delivery, forced reduced specialization, creation of unintentional overlaps over time.

### Problem 2: Rainbows

A single group spans incompatible Building Blocks, or a single person's job covers multiple unrelated disciplines. Named "rainbow" because when color-coded by Building Block type, the group shows multiple colors (like a rainbow).

Consequences: reduced specialization, conflicts of interest, poor performance, impossible-to-fill jobs.

### Problem 3: Scattered Campuses

A profession (Building Block type) fragmented across multiple separate groups throughout the organization. Examples: security expertise scattered in three different departments; application developers in both IT and Finance.

Consequences: reduces specialization, causes overlapping domains, redundant research, duplicated learning curves, inconsistent approaches.

### Problem 4: Inappropriate Substructure

Groups within a profession divided by the wrong basis (e.g., by clients' business processes instead of by technology specialty). Consequences as detailed in Principle 4.

## Special Situations

### Self-managed Groups

When a line of business is too small for a dedicated manager:
- Group manages itself as if there were a supervisor; supervisory duties shared among members.
- Choices for sharing: manager above does it; rotating "lead"; individuals independently; group consensus.
- Partial shared destiny required in performance appraisals (portion based on group performance).
- Caution: does not save headcount (supervisory duties still must be fulfilled); may reduce coherence and leadership strength; requires more attention from manager above.

### Shared People (Temporary Duty)

When an individual has skills spanning two domains:
- Lend individual to other group (part-time or full-time, temporary or ongoing).
- Receiving group manages borrowed individual; may pay salary; all accountability for work remains with receiving group.
- Individual has one "home" group; administrative manager handles HR issues; contributes to performance appraisal.
- Two managers must be clear about time allocation (e.g., 50/50 split with explicit scheduling).
- Not dual reporting (where two managers supervise one job); person works two distinct part-time jobs.

### Remote Locations

Four options:

| Option | Approach | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|--------|----------|------------|---------------|
| 1: Rainbow Groups | Autonomous "rainbow" group at location | Simplicity; minimal cross-geographic teamwork needed | All silo disadvantages; reduces specialization; sets bad precedent |
| 2: Site Coordinators | Specialists report to functional managers at central location; senior local person = site coordinator | Single boss; consistency in directions; professional participation | Requires clear delineation of site-coordinator vs. functional responsibilities |
| 3: Technician Services | Remote staff = "Technician Services" business; sell time, act as agents of central subject-matter experts | Works for field execution roles | Doesn't work for Account Sales or Coordinators |
| 4: Temporary Duty | Predominant function manages most staff; others loaned to other groups as temporary duty | Flexible | Requires careful time management |

Generally, location-specific rainbow groups should be avoided; with modern collaborative tools, rarely necessary.

### Project Management Office

**PMO problem pattern:** PMO accountable for projects → takes over delivery of other groups' products → disempowers subject-matter group → quality suffers (PMO optimizes delivery dates, not long-term integrity); subject-matter group becomes "body shop."

**Correct PMO role:** People-based Service Provider, accountable for delivering project planning and facilitation services—not project leadership or accountability for results. Helps everyone succeed at respective projects without assuming control.

## Teamwork Processes

Teamwork is not a luxury; it is an essential aspect of organizational design. Better teamwork enables more specialization, which enables better performance.

Effective cross-boundary teamwork does not result from team-building, job rotations, or process engineering alone. Root cause of teamwork failures is typically structural—silo organizations, unclear domains, process owners violating Golden Rule.

**What does work:** Explicit mechanisms for forming and coordinating teams, aligned with the principles of structure. Structural redesign itself creates the platform; specific workflow designs then enable cross-boundary collaboration.

## Benefits of Principle-based Structure and Cultural Transformation

### Efficiencies

- Overlaps eliminated: Time saved on redundant efforts and territorial disputes.
- Clear domains permit greater span: Groups self-directed; flatter organization; less supervisory workload.
- Professional collaboration encouraged: Shared specialties enable common research, faster learning, more reusable components.

Note: Efficiencies often reinvested in doing more with same budget (training, innovation, relationship building, quality improvements, addressing backlogs), not reduced budgets. Efficiencies develop gradually as structure settles, not instantly.

### Effectiveness

- Gaps eliminated: New lines of business deliver previously unavailable value.
- Specialization increases: Scattered specialties consolidated; more competent, more focused staff.
- Accountabilities for results clear: Results-orientation delivers results; clients know where to go; performance management becomes straightforward.
- Teamwork enhanced: Best talent from wide range of relevant specialties contributes to each project.
- Organization scalable: Easier to grow, take on new technologies, integrate acquisitions; clear where new opportunities fit; structure can last a lifetime.
- Executive bottleneck eliminated: Empowered staff require less micro-management; executives focus on strategic challenges.

### Cultural Transformation

**Empowerment:** Jobs defined by results → clear accountabilities → creative initiative → quality discipline (people accountable for maintaining what they sell; incentive to do things right the first time = core of Total Quality Management).

**Customer Focus:** Running businesses within business → funded to serve customers → focus on delivering products/services rather than bureaucratic territories.

**Entrepreneurship:** Groups defined as lines of business → everyone is entrepreneur → commitments kept → proactive value discovery → continuous cost and method optimization → strategic planning for future.

## Key Takeaways

- Structure matters; affects every aspect of organizational performance (efficiency, effectiveness, culture).
- Seven Principles provide scientific foundation: Golden Rule (authority = accountability), Specialization and Teamwork, Precise Domains, Basis for Substructure, Avoid Conflicts of Interests, Cluster by Professional Synergies, Business Within Business.
- Five Building Blocks (Engineers, Service Providers, Coordinators, Sales/Marketing, Audit) provide common language for analyzing and designing organizations.
- Rainbow Analysis diagnostic method reveals: Gaps, Rainbows, Scattered Campuses, Inappropriate Substructure.
- Teamwork processes are essential complement to the organization chart.
- Special situations can be addressed without compromising Principles.
- Well-designed structure lasts a lifetime, evolving naturally without need for major restructuring.
- Ultimate goal: organization where everyone is an empowered entrepreneur, collaborating seamlessly across boundaries, continually discovering new ways to add value.
